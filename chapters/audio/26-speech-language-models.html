32  Speech and Audio Language Models

Two lines of development run through the preceding chapters. One is about understanding: encoders trained by self-supervision (Chapter 27) or weak supervision (Section 28.8.1) that turn audio into representations a downstream head can read. The other is about generation: codecs (Chapter 30) that turn audio into tokens a language model can predict. This chapter is where they meet.

A speech language model — or, when the scope includes music and environmental sound, an audio language model — is a system in which a large language model consumes audio directly rather than a transcript of it, and in the fully general case emits audio directly too. The promise is straightforward. A transcript discards who is speaking, how they feel, whether they are joking, whether a dog is barking, and when they stopped talking. A model that hears the waveform need not discard any of it.

The reality is that these models are, at time of writing, noticeably less intelligent than the text LLMs they are built from. That gap is the organising question of this chapter, and it is more interesting than any individual architecture.

NoteReading order and scope

This chapter assumes Chapter 27 (encoders), Chapter 30 (tokenisers), and Section 31.9 (codec language modelling for synthesis). It deliberately does not re-derive AudioLM, VALL-E, or Moshi’s streaming mechanisms, which are treated as synthesis in Section 31.9.2, Section 31.9.3, and Section 31.9.6. Here they are components in a general-purpose model. The survey by Arora et al. (Arora et al. 2025) is the reference work for this material and is a better catalogue than this chapter attempts to be.

32.1 Three Architectures

Almost every system in this space is one of three things.

The cascade deserves more respect than it usually receives. It preserves the text LLM exactly — no forgetting, no degraded reasoning, full tool use and instruction following — and every component can be swapped, monitored, and tested independently. Its weaknesses are real but specific: latency accumulates, ASR errors propagate irreversibly, and everything not in the transcript is gone by the time the LLM sees anything. For a large class of assistant tasks that last weakness costs nothing, which is why cascades remain the default in production.

The interesting claim of the other two rows is that the discarded information matters. Testing that claim honestly is harder than it looks, and Section 32.7 returns to it.

32.2 The Adapter Recipe

The dominant architecture for audio understanding is a three-part sandwich, and it is worth writing down precisely because almost everything reduces to it.

Let \(\mathbf{X}\) be audio, \(f_\theta\) a pretrained audio encoder (Section 27.8.1), and \(\mathcal{P}_\phi\) a learned projector. The audio is converted to a sequence of vectors in the LLM’s embedding space and prepended to the text embeddings:

\[ \mathbf{H} = \mathcal{P}_\phi\big(f_\theta(\mathbf{X})\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times d_{\text{LLM}}}, \qquad p(\mathbf{y}) = \prod_{i} p_{\text{LLM}}\big(y_i \mid \mathbf{H}, \mathbf{e}(\mathbf{y}_{<i})\big) \tag{32.1}\]

The LLM is unmodified. It receives what it takes to be \(M\) ordinary token embeddings and continues as usual. This is the same trick that made vision-language models work, transplanted without alteration.

The projector’s real job is rate reduction, and the arithmetic explains most design choices. Whisper’s encoder emits 50 vectors per second; a one-minute clip is 3,000 positions, which would dominate any reasonable context. Projectors therefore downsample aggressively — by stacking adjacent frames before a linear map, by strided convolution, or by cross-attending a fixed number of learned queries into each window (a Q-Former). Typical operating points land between 5 and 12 vectors per second, a factor of 4–10 reduction.

Three systems established the design space.

SALMONN (Tang et al. 2024) uses two encoders in parallel — Whisper for speech and BEATs for general audio events — concatenated frame-by-frame and read by a window-level Q-Former, with LoRA adaptation of the LLM. The dual encoder is the point: a speech encoder trained on transcription is systematically poor at non-speech audio, which is exactly the WavLM/HuBERT specialisation of Section 27.3.2 reappearing one level up. SALMONN also reported task over-fitting — after instruction tuning, the model collapses toward the tasks it was tuned on and loses the emergent cross-modal abilities it briefly had — and proposed a lightweight activation-tuning stage to recover them. That failure recurs throughout this chapter.

Qwen-Audio (Chu et al. 2023) attacked the same problem from the data side, training one encoder across more than thirty tasks with a hierarchy of tags identifying task, language, and annotation granularity, so that supervision from heterogeneous datasets could be shared rather than interfering. Qwen2-Audio (Chu et al. 2024) dropped the tag hierarchy for natural-language prompts, and Qwen2.5-Omni (Xu et al. 2025) extended the same backbone to vision and to speech output.

LTU (Gong et al. 2024) made the case that the interesting capability is not classification but reasoning about audio — answering open-ended questions about what is happening and why — and built the instruction corpus to train it. Audio Flamingo (Kong et al. 2024; Ghosh et al. 2025) pursued in-context learning and long-audio understanding in the same spirit.

32.3 Training and Catastrophic Forgetting

Training proceeds in stages, and the recipe is stable across systems.

  1. Modality alignment. Freeze the LLM, train the projector alone on large-scale ASR and audio-captioning data. The objective is simply to make Equation 32.1 produce embeddings the LLM can read.
  2. Instruction tuning. Unfreeze partially — typically LoRA on the LLM, and often the encoder’s upper layers — and train on audio question-answering and multi-task instruction data.
  3. Speech output, where applicable: train the generation path (Section 31.9), usually with the understanding path frozen.
  4. Preference optimisation and duplex adaptation, increasingly standard.

The central difficulty is stage 2. Speech-centric training degrades the model’s text abilities, and the degradation is severe enough to be the principal reported obstacle. Speech-IFEval (Lu, Kuan, et al. 2025) isolates it by separating instruction-following from speech perception, and finds that most speech-aware LLMs “struggle with even basic instructions, performing far worse than text-based LLMs”, and are unstable under paraphrases of the same prompt. A model that hears well but can no longer follow a formatting instruction is not obviously an improvement on a cascade.

Two mitigations are in current use. The first is architectural conservatism: keep the LLM frozen and push all adaptation into the projector, which preserves text ability at the cost of how much audio detail can reach the model — Freeze-Omni (Wang et al. 2024) takes this position explicitly. The second is to fix the data. DeSTA2.5-Audio (Lu, Chen, et al. 2025) observes that forgetting is largely caused by training targets written in a style the backbone LLM would never produce, and has the backbone generate its own training targets from audio metadata, so that fine-tuning reinforces rather than overwrites its native distribution. Reported gains hold across Dynamic-SUPERB, MMAU, SAKURA, Speech-IFEval, and VoiceBench simultaneously, which is the kind of broad result that suggests a real mechanism rather than benchmark tuning.

WarningThe synthetic-data trap

Most audio instruction corpora are built by synthesising speech from text with a TTS system. Such data has no genuine paralinguistic variation — no real emotion, no real hesitation, no real room. A model trained on it can only learn that audio is a lossy encoding of text, which is precisely the failure the whole paradigm exists to avoid. This is a plausible mechanism behind Section 32.5, and it is self-inflicted.

32.4 Speaking: Interleaving, Streams, and Duplex

Models that emit audio face a decision about how text participates in generation. Nearly all of them use text somehow, and why is the interesting part.

Text-scaffolded generation is the near-universal pattern. Rather than modelling audio tokens alone, the model predicts text and audio jointly, with text leading:

\[ p(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{w}) = \prod_{t} p\big(w_t \mid \mathbf{w}_{<t}, \mathbf{a}_{<t}\big)\, p\big(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{w}_{\leq t}, \mathbf{a}_{<t}\big) \tag{32.2}\]

Two families implement Equation 32.2 differently. Interleaving alternates chunks of text and audio tokens in one sequence — the approach of SpeechGPT (Zhang et al. 2023), AudioPaLM (Rubenstein et al. 2023), and GLM-4-Voice (Zeng et al. 2024). Parallel streams predict text and audio at the same position on separate channels, which is Moshi’s inner monologue (Section 31.9.6). Mini-Omni (Xie and Wu 2024) and LLaMA-Omni (Fang et al. 2025) occupy points in between, generating text and audio concurrently to keep latency low.

The empirical finding is consistent and slightly deflating: the text stream carries most of the reasoning. Moshi’s ablations show the inner monologue improves linguistic quality substantially. Volhejn’s reading of this is that most of the intelligence is delegated to text while the audio streams degenerate into an integrated ASR and TTS — which, if true, means the architecture is a cascade wearing a different coat. I think that is too strong as a general claim, but it is the right worry, and no current system clearly refutes it.

Full duplex is the other axis. A half-duplex model processes one turn at a time; a full-duplex model listens and speaks simultaneously, which is what makes interruption, backchannelling (“mm-hm”), and overlap representable at all. Modelling both participants’ audio as parallel streams (Section 31.9.6) replaces an explicit turn-taking state machine with next-token prediction, and dGSLM (Nguyen et al. 2023) demonstrated the idea before speech LLMs existed by modelling two-channel spontaneous dialogue directly. Full-Duplex-Bench (Lin et al. 2025) gives the behaviours their own metrics: pause handling, backchannelling, turn-taking, and interruption management.

32.5 The Modality Gap

Here is the phenomenon, in the cleanest available form. Ask a natively speech-capable model, in a high-pitched voice, “Am I speaking in a low voice or a high voice?” It will generally fail — not by refusing, but by answering as though it had read a transcript. Volhejn reports this for Gemini, ChatGPT’s Advanced Voice Mode, Qwen, and Moshi alike.

This is a strange failure. The information is unambiguously present in the input, the model was trained on audio, and the question is trivial. Four mechanisms plausibly contribute, and they are not mutually exclusive.

Tokenisation discards it by construction. Semantic tokens are defined by invariance to speaker and channel — the demonstration in Section 30.7, where fixing semantic tokens and resampling the rest yields the same words in a different voice, shows this directly. A model whose loss is dominated by semantic tokens is trained to ignore exactly the cues in question. The layer-wise result of Section 27.5 says the same thing about encoders: representations optimised for content actively suppress paralinguistic nuisance variables.

Text scaffolding makes audio vestigial. If Equation 32.2 lets the model route all reasoning through text, gradient pressure on the audio path to encode anything beyond phonetic content is weak.

The training data has no variation to learn from. The synthetic-data trap above.

Scale asymmetry. Text corpora are trillions of tokens; transcribed and instruction-annotated speech is orders of magnitude smaller, and the annotated paralinguistic slice smaller still.

The measurements support a sharper diagnosis than “the model cannot hear”. SAKURA (Yang et al. 2025) evaluates multi-hop reasoning over speech and audio and finds that models fail to integrate audio information into a reasoning chain even when they extract the relevant information correctly. Perception is not the bottleneck; integration is. Consistently, a 2026 layer-wise study (Yang et al. 2026) finds that paralinguistic and semantic information occupy identifiable and distinct layers of these models, and that fine-tuning selected layers with an auxiliary classification head restores paralinguistic awareness more effectively than fine-tuning everything. The information is present and localisable; the model is simply not in the habit of using it.

That is a more optimistic reading than the phenomenon first suggests. It says the gap is a training and objective-design problem rather than a representational impossibility.

32.6 The Tasks That Became Prompts

A striking side-effect of this architecture is that a set of previously separate research areas collapsed into prompting. Each once had its own models, losses, and leaderboards; each is now a question you ask.

Two caveats keep this from being the whole story. Specialised models still win on their own tasks, often by a wide margin, and the unified model’s advantage is breadth and compositionality rather than peak accuracy; Chapter 33 takes these tasks on their own terms, including the measurement problems the prompting framing inherits. And contrastive audio-text models remain a distinct and more efficient paradigm for retrieval-style problems: CLAP (Elizalde et al. 2023) aligns audio and text in a shared embedding space, giving zero-shot classification over an open label set at a fraction of the cost of generative inference.

32.7 Evaluation

Evaluating these models is harder than evaluating either parent, because the output is open-ended text about an open-ended input.

Three methodological problems are worth naming, because they affect how the numbers in these tables should be read.

Many questions are answerable from the transcript alone. If a benchmark item can be solved by an ASR-plus-text-LLM cascade, it measures nothing about audio understanding. The correct control is to run that cascade and report it as a baseline — a practice that is becoming standard but is still often omitted. Any claim that native audio processing helps should be a claim about the gap to that baseline.

Open-ended answers are usually scored by an LLM judge, which inherits the judge’s biases, is sensitive to answer formatting, and has received little meta-evaluation in this domain. This is the same problem flagged for synthesis in Section 31.12, and it is not better here.

Perception and instruction-following are routinely conflated. A model can fail an item because it did not hear correctly or because it ignored the output format. Speech-IFEval exists precisely to separate the two, and the finding that much of the apparent perception failure is really instruction failure should make one cautious about single-number leaderboards.

32.8 Open Problems

  1. Closing the modality gap. The evidence in Section 32.5 suggests paralinguistic information is present but unused. Whether the fix is objective design, better data, selective adaptation, or richer tokenisation is the field’s central open question.
  2. Forgetting without a trade-off. Every current method buys text ability with audio detail or vice versa. A training scheme that adds hearing without subtracting reasoning does not yet exist.
  3. Beating the cascade convincingly. On most benchmarks a strong cascade remains competitive. Establishing where native processing genuinely wins — and by how much — is unresolved, and partly an evaluation problem.
  4. Long-form audio. Rate reduction in Equation 32.1 makes minutes tractable and hours not. Meetings, lectures, and podcasts remain out of reach without chunking that destroys global structure.
  5. Duplex behaviour is barely modelled. Knowing when to speak, when to stay silent, and when to yield is a skill with almost no supervision available, and Full-Duplex-Bench shows current systems handle it crudely.
  6. Speaker attribution in multi-party audio. These models largely assume one speaker. Integrating diarization (Chapter 29) into a speech LLM, rather than bolting it on, is open.
  7. Safety at the paralinguistic layer. A model that can hear emotion, health markers, age, and identity can also infer them without consent, and a model that can speak in any voice can impersonate. Neither the inference side nor the generation side has an accepted governance story.
  8. Scaling laws. Nobody has mapped how audio-language capability trades off between audio hours, text tokens, parameters, and token rate.

32.9 Summary

Speech and audio language models are the convergence point of this part of the book: the encoders of Chapter 27 supply perception, the codecs of Chapter 30 supply a generation interface, and a pretrained text LLM supplies everything else. Three architectures divide the space — the cascade, which preserves text ability perfectly and discards everything not in the transcript; the adapter sandwich of Equation 32.1, which projects encoder features into the LLM’s embedding space and is essentially the vision-language recipe unchanged; and native token-in-token-out models that read and write audio directly.

The recurring technical difficulty is that teaching a language model to hear tends to make it worse at thinking. Instruction tuning on audio degrades text ability measurably, task-specific tuning collapses emergent generality, and the mitigations — frozen backbones, selective adaptation, self-generated training targets — all trade one capability against another.

The recurring scientific difficulty is the modality gap. A model that hears a high-pitched voice and cannot say so is failing at something its input contains and its architecture permits. The most useful recent finding is that this is not a perception failure: the information is extractable, and localisable to particular layers, but does not make it into the model’s reasoning. Tokenisers that define away paralinguistics, text scaffolding that renders the audio path vestigial, and synthetic training data with no genuine acoustic variation all push in the same direction. That the causes are identifiable and largely self-inflicted is the best reason to expect the gap to close.

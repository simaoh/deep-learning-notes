33  Audio Understanding Beyond Words

Every chapter so far has treated audio as a carrier of words. Recognition extracts them, synthesis produces them, codecs compress the signal that contains them, and speech language models reason about them. This chapter is about everything else in the signal.

That residue divides into two families. Paralinguistic attributes are properties of the speaker and the delivery rather than the message: emotion, age, accent, health, arousal, whether the utterance is sincere. Non-speech audio is the rest of the acoustic world: a door closing, a siren, rain, a violin. They are conventionally separate research communities with separate venues, and this chapter treats them together because they share a difficulty that the tasks in earlier chapters do not have.

That difficulty is the label. Word error rate works because a transcript has a correct answer, and disagreement between annotators is rare and resolvable. Ask two people whether a speaker sounds angry or merely emphatic, or to describe a five-second recording in a sentence, and they will disagree — not through carelessness, but because the question does not have a single answer. Almost everything distinctive about this area follows from that.

NoteReading order

This chapter assumes Chapter 27 (encoders and layer-wise specialisation) and touches Section 32.6, which noted that these tasks are increasingly posed as prompts to an audio language model. That framing is where the field is going; this chapter is about what it is absorbing, and about the measurement problems it inherits in doing so.

33.1 The Annotation Problem

Consider what a “ground truth” emotion label is. An annotator listens to an utterance and selects a category. A second annotator hears the same utterance and selects a different one. Neither is wrong. The label that reaches the dataset is usually the majority vote, which discards the disagreement and presents the result as though it were fact.

Three consequences follow, and they recur through every section below.

Reported accuracy is bounded by human agreement, which for categorical emotion is frequently in the range of 0.3–0.6 by chance-corrected measures such as Krippendorff’s \(\alpha\). A model reporting 75% accuracy against majority-vote labels on a corpus where annotators agree 60% of the time is reporting something other than what the number appears to say.

The majority vote is a lossy summary of a distribution. A clip where seven of ten annotators say angry and three say frustrated is genuinely different from one where ten of ten say angry, and collapsing both to angry throws away the distinction. Soft labels — training against the annotator distribution with a cross-entropy or divergence objective rather than a one-hot target — are the standard response, and are now common practice in emotion and sound-event work alike.

Evaluation must respect the same fact. Scoring a prediction against one reference when many are valid is the same structural problem as MOS in synthesis (Section 31.12.1) and as the one-to-many problem in generation (Section 31.2). It is the reason audio captioning needed its own metrics rather than borrowing image captioning’s, as Section 33.5 describes.

33.2 Audio Classification Backbones

The non-speech side of the field was organised by one dataset. AudioSet (Gemmeke et al. 2017) is roughly two million ten-second YouTube clips labelled against an ontology of 527 sound classes, and it played the role for audio that ImageNet played for vision: large enough that pre-training on it transfers, and standard enough that everyone reports on it.

Two observations are worth drawing out. AST’s initialisation from ImageNet weights works despite a spectrogram not being an image in any meaningful sense — its axes are time and frequency, not two spatial dimensions, and translation invariance along frequency is wrong for audio, since shifting a harmonic stack upward changes the sound. That it transfers anyway says something about how generic early ViT features are.

And BEATs closes a loop this book has traced twice. It is HuBERT’s insight — that the quality of a masked-prediction target can be improved by re-deriving it from the model you just trained — applied to general audio rather than speech (Section 27.3.1). The same idea reappears in codecs as semantic distillation (Section 30.7).

33.3 Weak Labels and Temporal Localisation

AudioSet labels are clip-level: a ten-second clip is marked as containing a dog bark, with no indication of when. Sound event detection requires the onset and offset. This is a mismatch between the supervision available and the output required, and it is the technical core of the non-speech side.

The standard formulation is multiple-instance learning. The model produces a frame-level probability \(y_t\) for each class and pools it to a clip-level prediction compared against the weak label. Max pooling is degenerate — it propagates gradient to one frame — and mean pooling dilutes short events, so the usual choice is attention pooling, in which the model learns its own weighting:

\[ \hat{y} = \frac{\sum_{t} y_t \, w_t}{\sum_{t} w_t}, \qquad w_t = \sigma\big(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{h}_t\big) \tag{33.1}\]

The frame-level \(y_t\) is never directly supervised; it emerges as the byproduct of explaining a clip-level label. That is an elegant trick and also a fragile one, since nothing forces \(y_t\) to be temporally accurate as long as the pooled value is right.

The DCASE challenge’s long-running Task 4 formalised the harder realistic setting: a small strongly-labelled set, a larger weakly-labelled set, and a large unlabelled set. The standard method is mean teacher (Tarvainen and Valpola 2017) — an exponential moving average of the student’s weights supplies consistency targets on unlabelled audio — imported directly from semi-supervised image classification, and structurally the same EMA-teacher device that data2vec uses for representation learning (Section 27.3.3).

Evaluation has its own subtlety. Event-based F-score with a fixed tolerance (“collar”) around each boundary depends heavily on an arbitrary collar width and on a chosen operating threshold. The polyphonic sound detection score (Bilen et al. 2020) replaces both with a threshold-independent, collar-free formulation that integrates performance across operating points and explicitly separates confusion between classes from missed detections. It is now the standard DCASE metric, and the reasoning behind it — that a metric which moves when you change an arbitrary hyperparameter is measuring the hyperparameter — generalises well beyond this task.

33.4 Speech Emotion Recognition

Emotion is represented in two incompatible ways, and much confusion in this literature comes from comparing across them.

Categorical representations assign a discrete label from a small set (angry, happy, sad, neutral). They are intuitive and align with how people talk about emotion, but the categories are culturally specific and the boundaries are not sharp. Dimensional representations place an utterance in a continuous space, conventionally valence (pleasant–unpleasant), arousal (calm–excited), and sometimes dominance. They avoid forcing discrete choices and are the more defensible target, and they are scored with the concordance correlation coefficient, which rewards agreement in both correlation and absolute scale:

\[ \rho_c = \frac{2\,\rho\,\sigma_x \sigma_y}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + (\mu_x - \mu_y)^2} \tag{33.2}\]

Categorical work reports weighted and unweighted accuracy, the latter averaging per-class recall; the distinction matters because emotion corpora are severely imbalanced toward neutral.

Arousal is easy and valence is hard. Arousal correlates strongly with straightforward acoustics — energy, pitch range, speaking rate — so it was largely solved early. Valence does not: whether an utterance is pleasant or unpleasant often depends on what is being said. This “valence gap” defined the field for a decade.

The most instructive result here is that transformers closed the gap, and why. Wagner et al. (Wagner et al. 2023) fine-tuned wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT variants on MSP-Podcast and obtained state-of-the-art valence prediction (CCC 0.638) without any explicit linguistic input. Their analysis of the mechanism is the part worth teaching: the models succeed because the transformer layers learn implicit linguistic information during fine-tuning, which is why they match multimodal systems that use transcripts explicitly. The model closed the valence gap by covertly learning to read.

That finding is a useful corrective in two directions. It explains an apparent capability without crediting the model with acoustic sensitivity it does not have, and it connects directly to Section 32.5: a system that appears to hear emotion may be routing through language, which is the same substitution that makes audio language models insensitive to pitch.

The same study is unusually careful about robustness and fairness, and reports a distinction worth carrying: the models were fair with respect to biological sex groups but not with respect to individual speakers. Aggregate group parity and per-speaker reliability are different properties, and a system can have the first without the second.

WarningIEMOCAP results are frequently not comparable

IEMOCAP has been the field’s default corpus for fifteen years, and papers using it differ on choices that change results by many points: whether to merge excited into happy to reach the conventional four classes, whether to use improvised or scripted sessions or both, and whether the split is speaker-independent. A great many reported comparisons are therefore between different tasks wearing the same name. MSP-Podcast, larger and drawn from natural speech, is the better modern default.

33.5 Audio Captioning and Language-Aligned Audio

Automated audio captioning asks for a free-text description of a clip — “a dog barks twice as a car passes” — which is the general-audio analogue of the open-ended understanding that audio language models aim at. The standard corpora are AudioCaps and Clotho (Drossos et al. 2020), the latter deliberately multi-reference, with five captions per clip.

Captioning inherited its metrics from image captioning, and they did not transfer. Zhou et al. (Zhou et al. 2022) built human-judgement benchmarks for audio captions — using pairwise comparisons rather than absolute ratings, precisely because pairwise judgements achieve better annotator agreement — and found that SPICE, CIDEr, and SPIDEr (Liu et al. 2017) correlate poorly with human assessment. Their replacement, FENSE, combines Sentence-BERT similarity with a learned error detector that penalises fluent but incorrect sentences, and improves agreement with human judgement by 14–25%. The methodological lesson is the same one Section 31.12 reaches from a different direction: a borrowed metric requires validation in its new domain, and this one was used for years without it.

33.5.1 Contrastive Audio-Text Models

The other way to connect audio to language is not to generate text but to align the two in a shared embedding space. CLAP (Elizalde et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2023) applies the CLIP recipe to audio: an audio encoder \(f\) and a text encoder \(g\) trained on paired data with a symmetric contrastive objective over a batch,

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \left[ \log \frac{e^{\,s(a_i, t_i)/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{\,s(a_i, t_j)/\tau}} + \log \frac{e^{\,s(a_i, t_i)/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{\,s(a_j, t_i)/\tau}} \right] \tag{33.3}\]

with \(s(a,t) = \cos\big(f(a), g(t)\big)\) and temperature \(\tau\).

What this buys is an open label set. Classification becomes ranking a set of text prompts against the audio embedding, so the classes need not be fixed at training time and adding a new one costs nothing. It also gives text-to-audio retrieval directly, and — as Section 31.12 noted for FAD — a general-purpose audio embedding space that turns out to correlate with human perceptual judgement better than codec latents do.

CLAP and audio language models are complementary rather than competing. CLAP is a single forward pass per candidate and is the right tool for retrieval, tagging, and filtering at scale; a generative model is the right tool when the answer is a sentence rather than a ranking.

33.6 Other Paralinguistic Inferences

A short catalogue, since these share the machinery above and differ mainly in what is being inferred and at what risk.

Speaker traits — age, sex, accent, native language — are standard classification over speaker embeddings (Section 29.1.1) or SSL features. Speech pathology and health covers dysarthria, Parkinsonian speech, and a large and methodologically contested literature on inferring depression and cognitive decline. Spoofing and deepfake detection is the countermeasure side of Section 31.10: the ASVspoof series (Wang et al. 2024) is the standard evaluation, and its central finding across editions is that detectors generalise poorly to attack types they were not trained on — which is why the synthesis chapter treats passive detection as substantially unsolved.

Two of these have a property the others do not: the inference is about a person, and can be made without their knowledge. That is the subject of Section 33.8.

33.7 Evaluation

HEAR is worth singling out for its design. It freezes the encoder and varies only a shallow downstream head across a deliberately heterogeneous task set, which tests generality rather than task-specific tuning — the same protocol as SUPERB, and it revealed the same thing: no single encoder wins everywhere, and speech encoders and general-audio encoders have genuinely different strengths. That result is what forces SALMONN’s dual-encoder design (Section 32.2).

33.8 Validity, Fairness, and Regulation

This is the one area in this book where the most important open problem is not technical.

Construct validity. Supervised learning assumes the label denotes something real and stable. For emotion that assumption is contested within psychology itself. Barrett et al. (Barrett et al. 2019) reviewed the evidence on inferring emotional state from facial movements and concluded that the mapping is far weaker and more context-dependent than the “universal expression” model assumes. Their review concerns faces rather than voice, so it is not direct evidence about speech — but the argument it makes is structural, about whether a fixed set of expressions reliably signals a fixed set of internal states, and it applies to vocal expression for the same reasons. A model can be an excellent predictor of annotator labels while those labels are a poor measure of what the speaker felt, and no amount of accuracy resolves the difference.

Fairness. Systems inherit the demographic composition of their training data, which for paralinguistics is usually unknown and rarely balanced. The distinction from Section 33.4 is the operative one: parity across broad groups does not imply reliability for individuals, and it is individuals who are affected.

Regulation. The EU AI Act prohibits placing on the market or using AI systems that infer emotions in the workplace and in education, outside narrow medical and safety exceptions, and treats emotion recognition as high-risk in other contexts. This is one of the few places where a research area in this book has been directly restricted by law, and the stated rationale is the validity concern above rather than a general objection to the technology. Anyone building in this area should read the operative text rather than a summary of it, including this one.

The practical position I would take is that these systems are defensible for aggregate, consented, low-stakes uses — retrieval, content description, accessibility, research on populations — and difficult to defend for individual consequential decisions about people who did not consent to being measured.

33.9 Open Problems

  1. Labels that reflect uncertainty. Soft labels are a partial answer. Training and evaluation protocols that treat annotator disagreement as signal rather than noise are still not standard.
  2. Robustness across corpora. Cross-corpus emotion recognition degrades sharply, which suggests models learn corpus-specific recording and elicitation artefacts alongside the target attribute.
  3. Disentangling acoustics from language. The Section 33.4 finding that valence performance rests on implicit lexical learning means we do not currently know how much genuinely acoustic affect recognition is achievable.
  4. Weak supervision without temporal degradation. Equation 33.1 produces frame-level outputs that nothing supervises; better inductive biases or cheap forms of temporal supervision remain open.
  5. Generalisation of spoofing countermeasures to unseen attacks, which every ASVspoof edition has identified and none has closed.
  6. Polyphony and overlap. Real acoustic scenes contain many simultaneous sources; most benchmarks under-represent dense overlap.
  7. Do audio LLMs subsume this? They are broad and weak; specialised models are narrow and strong. Whether the general model eventually dominates, as it did in text, is unresolved — and Section 32.5 suggests the paralinguistic part is exactly where it currently does not.

33.10 Summary

What remains in an audio signal after the words have been removed is the subject of two research communities that share one problem: their labels are constructions rather than facts. Annotators disagree irreducibly about emotion and about how to describe a sound, so majority-vote targets discard real information, reported accuracies live under a ceiling set by human agreement, and metrics borrowed from domains with unique correct answers mislead.

The non-speech side was organised by AudioSet and by a progression of backbones — PANNs, AST, BEATs — whose trajectory recapitulates the rest of the book: convolutional to transformer, supervised to self-supervised, and finally to iterative masked prediction over learned discrete targets, which is HuBERT’s idea transplanted to general audio. Its distinctive technical problem is supervision that is weaker than the required output, addressed by multiple-instance learning with attention pooling and by semi-supervised consistency training, and evaluated by a score designed not to move when you change an arbitrary threshold.

The speech side’s clearest lesson is a negative one. Transformers closed the long-standing valence gap in emotion recognition, but the mechanism was implicit lexical learning rather than acoustic sensitivity — the model learned to read. That is the same substitution of language for audio diagnosed in Section 32.5, appearing here in a task where it was measured precisely, and it should make one cautious about any claim that a model has learned to hear something when reading would also have sufficed.

Finally, this is the part of the field where the binding constraint is not architecture or data but whether the quantity being predicted exists as labelled. That question is unresolved in psychology, and it has now been answered provisionally in law.

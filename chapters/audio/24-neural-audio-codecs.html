30  Neural Audio Codecs

Section 31.9 treated the token grid \(\mathbf{C} \in \{1..V\}^{T_c \times N_q}\) as given. This chapter opens the box.

A neural audio codec is a learned compressor: an encoder that maps a waveform to a low-rate latent sequence, a quantiser that maps each latent frame to one or more discrete indices, and a decoder that reconstructs the waveform from those indices. It is trained end-to-end, usually with adversarial supervision, and it plays two quite different roles depending on who is using it. To a communications engineer it is a codec in the classical sense — a way to transmit speech at 1.5 kbps instead of 256 kbps. To someone building a speech language model it is a tokeniser: the thing that turns a continuous signal into a sequence of integers so that the machinery of language modelling applies.

Those two roles pull in different directions, and much of what follows is about that tension. A codec optimised purely for reconstruction produces tokens that are hard to model; a codec optimised for modelling gives up fidelity. Nearly every design decision in this chapter — frame rate, codebook count, semantic distillation, quantiser structure — is a position on that trade-off.

NoteReading order

This chapter assumes Chapter 31 (which motivates codecs) and Section 27.8.2 (which supplies the semantic-token half of the story). It is a prerequisite for the speech language models of Chapter 32. Readers who want the image-domain background will find VQ-VAE and VQGAN familiar (Oord et al. 2017; Esser et al. 2021); this chapter re-derives what it needs.

30.1 Why Audio Needs a Tokeniser

The motivating problem is a rate mismatch, and it is worth quantifying because the numbers are what force the design.

One second of English speech is roughly three to four text tokens. The same second at 24 kHz is 24,000 samples. If you tokenise audio at the sample level — \(\mu\)-law companding to 256 levels, as WaveNet did (Section 31.5.1) — a language model with a 2,048-token context window sees 128 milliseconds of audio at 16 kHz, which is less than one word. No amount of training fixes a context window that cannot hold a single word.

This is not a thought experiment. Volhejn’s Kyutai explainer runs it directly: a 151M-parameter GPT-2-scale Transformer trained on 1,000 hours of Libri-Light with sample-level \(\mu\)-law tokens produces speech-like babbling in which no word is intelligible, the voice drifts, and generation of ten seconds takes about thirty minutes on an H100. The failure is not acoustic — locally the audio sounds like speech — it is a failure of coherence over time, exactly as predicted by the context-window arithmetic.

So we compress. If the encoder downsamples by a factor of \(D\) and emits \(N_q\) tokens per frame, the token rate is

\[ \text{tokens/s} = \underbrace{\frac{f_s}{D}}_{\text{frame rate } f_r} \times N_q, \qquad \text{bitrate } R = f_r \cdot N_q \cdot \log_2 V \; \text{bits/s} \tag{30.1}\]

where \(V\) is the codebook size. Equation 30.1 is the budget every architecture in this chapter negotiates with, so it is worth grounding in real numbers:

Two observations follow immediately, and they organise the rest of the chapter. Frame rate is the most valuable axis for a language model, because it reduces sequence length linearly — Mimi’s 12.5 Hz is a sixfold advantage over EnCodec’s 75 Hz before any other consideration. And \(N_q\) is a liability rather than an asset for a language model, because a model emits one token at a time and \(N_q\) tokens per frame must be serialised somehow (Section 30.8). Compression research wants high \(N_q\); modelling research wants \(N_q = 1\).

30.2 From Autoencoders to VQ-VAE

The cleanest way into vector quantisation is to leave audio entirely, which is the route the Kyutai explainer takes and which I follow here because the intuition transfers exactly.

Train an ordinary autoencoder on images — say Fashion-MNIST — compressing each image to a two-dimensional latent. Plotting the latent space shows it clusters naturally by class without ever being told the classes. Now quantise: maintain a list of cluster centres (a codebook) \(\mathcal{E} = \{\mathbf{e}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_V\}\), assign each latent to its nearest centre,

\[ k = \arg\min_{v \in \{1..V\}} \big\| \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{e}_v \big\|_2, \qquad \mathbf{z}_q = \mathbf{e}_k \tag{30.2}\]

and hand \(\mathbf{z}_q\) rather than \(\mathbf{z}\) to the decoder. The consequence is immediately visible in the reconstructions: two images whose latents land in the same cell decode to exactly the same output. Quantisation is lossy in a very concrete, inspectable way.

30.2.1 The Straight-Through Estimator

Equation 30.2 contains an \(\arg\min\), which has zero gradient almost everywhere. Training would stop at the quantiser.

The fix, in Volhejn’s memorable phrasing, is to pretend the problem does not exist. Treat \(\mathbf{z}_q\) as \(\mathbf{z}\) displaced by a constant, and copy the decoder’s gradient past the quantiser unchanged:

\[ \mathbf{z}_q = \mathbf{z} + \operatorname{sg}\!\big[\mathbf{e}_k - \mathbf{z}\big], \qquad \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_q}{\partial \mathbf{z}} := \mathbf{I} \tag{30.3}\]

where \(\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]\) is the stop-gradient operator. The forward pass is unchanged; the backward pass behaves as though quantisation never happened. This is the straight-through estimator.

There is a price, and it is worth stating because it explains the next ingredient. The encoder is updated as if its output went to the decoder unquantised, so the gradient direction is wrong by roughly the quantisation error. The approximation is good only when latents sit close to their assigned centres — which gives us a reason to make them sit close.

30.2.2 Commitment, Codebook Updates, and Dead Codes

The full VQ-VAE objective (Oord et al. 2017) has three terms:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{VQ}} = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \underbrace{\big\| \operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{z}] - \mathbf{e}_k \big\|_2^2}_{\text{codebook}} + \beta \underbrace{\big\| \mathbf{z} - \operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{e}_k] \big\|_2^2}_{\text{commitment}} \tag{30.4}\]

The codebook term pulls each code toward the mean of the latents assigned to it — gradient-descent \(k\)-means. The commitment term pulls the encoder toward the codes, penalising latents that stray far from their centre; this is precisely what keeps the straight-through approximation honest. The two terms are mirror images distinguished only by where the stop-gradient sits.

The codebook term is often replaced by an exponential moving average update (Razavi et al. 2019), which is more stable and is what DAC, Mimi, and most modern codecs use. With \(n_v\) the count of latents assigned to code \(v\) in a batch and \(\mathbf{m}_v\) their sum,

\[ N_v \leftarrow \mu N_v + (1-\mu) n_v, \qquad \mathbf{M}_v \leftarrow \mu \mathbf{M}_v + (1-\mu)\mathbf{m}_v, \qquad \mathbf{e}_v \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{M}_v}{N_v} \tag{30.5}\]

with \(\mu \approx 0.99\)–\(0.999\). Under an EMA update the codebook is no longer trained by the loss at all, so only the commitment term in Equation 30.4 contributes to the generator gradient.

Dead codes are the characteristic pathology. A code that is never selected receives no update — under gradient descent or EMA alike — and therefore can never move to where the data is. It is stranded permanently. The standard remedy is blunt and effective: if a code goes unused for some number of steps, reinitialise it to a randomly chosen latent from the current batch. Volhejn describes this as teleporting the centre, which is exactly right. Dead-code reinitialisation appears in almost every production codec, usually with a threshold of a few hundred to a few thousand steps of disuse.

30.3 Residual Vector Quantisation

A single codebook of size \(V\) contributes \(\log_2 V\) bits per frame. To get 20 bits you would need \(2^{20} \approx 10^6\) entries, which is prohibitive in memory and in nearest-neighbour search cost, and hopeless statistically since most entries would rarely be selected.

Residual vector quantisation obtains the same expressiveness from a cascade of small codebooks by quantising what the previous stage got wrong. Recall from Section 30.2.1 that the quantisation error \(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{e}_k\) is already sitting there in the straight-through computation. Quantise it, with a second codebook, and repeat:

\[ \mathbf{r}_0 = \mathbf{z}, \qquad \mathbf{q}_n = Q_n(\mathbf{r}_{n-1}), \qquad \mathbf{r}_n = \mathbf{r}_{n-1} - \mathbf{q}_n, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{z}} = \sum_{n=1}^{N_q} \mathbf{q}_n \tag{30.6}\]

where \(Q_n\) is nearest-neighbour lookup in the \(n\)-th codebook. Two codebooks of 1,024 entries give \(2^{20}\) effective combinations from 2,048 stored vectors.

def rvq_quantize(z, codebooks):
    residual, codes = z, []
    for cb in codebooks:
        quantized, index = nearest_code(cb, residual)
        residual = residual - quantized
        codes.append(index)
    return codes            # one integer per level

Three properties matter downstream.

The levels are ordered by importance. Codebook 1 quantises the signal itself and therefore carries the coarse structure; each subsequent codebook encodes a progressively smaller correction. This is not a designed hierarchy but a consequence of Equation 30.6, and it is what makes the semantic/acoustic split of Section 30.7 possible.

Bitrate is truncatable at inference. Decoding with the first \(n < N_q\) codebooks yields a valid, lower-quality reconstruction at a lower bitrate, with no retraining. This only works well if training prepared for it, which is what RVQ dropout does: randomly truncate the number of levels used during training so the decoder never learns to depend on all of them. SoundStream introduced this as “quantizer dropout” and Mimi uses it; a codec trained without it must be retrained for each operating point.

The output is a two-dimensional grid, \(T_c \times N_q\), which is the problem of Section 30.8.

RVQ predates neural codecs by decades — it is standard in classical vector quantisation — and reached audio through SoundStream (Zeghidour et al. 2022). The same idea reached image generation independently (Lee et al. 2022).

30.4 Codebook Collapse and How to Avoid It

Codebook collapse is the recurring failure of this chapter, and the reader has met it twice already: Equation 27.4 was wav2vec 2.0’s answer to it, and CosyVoice 2’s move to scalar quantisation (Section 31.9.4) was another. It deserves a proper treatment here because codecs are where it bites hardest.

The failure is that a small fraction of codes absorb most of the probability mass while the rest go unused. Effective bitrate then falls below the nominal \(\log_2 V\) bits per level: you are paying for a 1,024-entry codebook and using 200 of them. Four families of fix are in current use.

EMA plus dead-code reinitialisation (Section 30.2.2) is the baseline and is nearly universal.

Factorised and \(L_2\)-normalised codes, introduced by DAC (Kumar et al. 2023), are the most cited fix. The idea is to perform the codebook lookup in a low-dimensional projected space (DAC uses 8 or 32 dimensions against a 1,024-dimensional latent) and to \(L_2\)-normalise both latents and codes so that lookup becomes cosine similarity on a hypersphere. Both changes make the nearest-neighbour geometry better conditioned and dramatically improve utilisation. This is the single most transferable trick in codec engineering.

Finite scalar quantisation (Mentzer et al. 2024) eliminates the problem structurally rather than treating it. Project the latent to a low dimension \(d\) (typically 3–8), then round each dimension independently to one of \(L\) levels after a bounding nonlinearity:

\[ \hat{z}_i = \operatorname{round}\!\big( \lfloor L/2 \rfloor \tanh(z_i) \big), \qquad \text{index} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \hat{z}_i' \, L^{\,i-1}, \qquad V = L^d \tag{30.7}\]

There is no codebook — the “codes” are the vertices of a fixed integer lattice, every one of which is reachable by construction. No commitment loss, no EMA, no dead-code reinitialisation, no auxiliary entropy term. Gradients use a straight-through round. FSQ matches learned VQ at comparable vocabulary sizes while removing an entire category of failure, which is why it has spread quickly into speech systems.

Single large codebook with careful engineering is the fourth route. BigCodec (Xin et al. 2024) and WavTokenizer (Ji et al. 2025) abandon RVQ for one high-cardinality codebook, betting that the modelling simplification is worth the engineering difficulty. X-Codec2, the tokeniser behind Llasa (Ye et al. 2025), reports roughly 99% utilisation of a 65,536-entry codebook at 50 Hz — evidence that the collapse problem is tractable even at extreme vocabulary sizes given the right lookup geometry.

30.4.1 Per-Layer Entropy as a Diagnostic

Collapse is usually reported as a single utilisation percentage, which hides where it happens. A more informative diagnostic is the entropy of the code distribution at each RVQ level, \(H_n = -\sum_v p_{n,v}\log_2 p_{n,v}\), compared against the ceiling \(\log_2 V\).

A recent study of tonal content in codecs (Mondal and Gupta 2026) reports such profiles and finds the deficit concentrated in the upper layers: DAC at 32 kbps falls below 7.0 bits (of a possible 10) for codebook indices above 24, and EnCodec at 24 kbps is worse. The proposed explanation is a data argument rather than an optimisation one — upper layers encode fine residual detail, and the signals that generate rich fine residuals (sustained harmonic material: bells, glockenspiel, piano) are under-represented in ordinary training corpora, so the upper codebooks are starved of the very data that would populate them. Rebalancing training toward such content raised reported active-entry usage from about 74% to about 95%.

The same work gives a usefully crisp definition of a content-dependent codec failure — one that aggregate metrics hide. Tonal weakness decomposes into harmonic artefacts (spurious components near harmonics), decay truncation (premature clipping of long amplitude envelopes, giving an unnatural staccato), and pitch instability (wobble on nominally steady tones). The DAC and EnCodec papers both acknowledge degradation on this material without offering a remedy.

WarningHow much weight to put on this

The tonal study is a limited-scale result and says so: 720 hours of training audio on four GPUs, compared against public DAC checkpoints the authors estimate used 10–50\(\times\) more data, with a 22-listener panel and large-scale replication left as future work. Take the diagnostic — per-layer entropy, and the observation that content under-represented in training starves the upper codebooks — rather than the specific model or its benchmark numbers.

30.5 Training Objectives

A codec is trained with a composite objective that inherits almost entirely from the GAN vocoders of Section 31.5.2. Nothing here is conceptually new; what is new is the quantiser sitting in the middle.

Multi-scale spectral reconstruction. An \(L_1\) distance between log-mel spectrograms of reference and reconstruction, summed over several STFT window sizes:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}} = \sum_{i \in S} \big\| \log \operatorname{Mel}_i(\mathbf{x}) - \log \operatorname{Mel}_i(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \big\|_1, \qquad S = \{32, 64, \ldots, 2048\} \tag{30.8}\]

Multiple resolutions are used because a single STFT window fixes one point on the time-frequency resolution trade-off, and a model optimised against it can exploit the blind spot. Short windows see transients; long windows see harmonic structure and long decays.

Adversarial and feature-matching terms. A bank of sub-discriminators \(\{D_k\}\) — multi-period on the raw waveform (Section 31.5.2) plus multi-scale or multi-band STFT discriminators — trained with a hinge objective, alongside an \(L_1\) match between the discriminators’ intermediate activations on real and reconstructed audio:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} = \sum_{k} \mathbb{E}\big[\max(0,\, 1 - D_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))\big], \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{fm}} = \sum_{k}\sum_{l} \frac{1}{N_{k,l}} \big\| D_k^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}) - D_k^{(l)}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \big\|_1 \tag{30.9}\]

Quantisation terms. The commitment loss of Equation 30.4, summed over RVQ levels.

The total is a weighted sum, and the weights are lopsided in a way worth noticing:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{spec}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}} + \lambda_{\text{fm}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{fm}} + \lambda_{\text{adv}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} + \lambda_{\text{commit}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{commit}} \tag{30.10}\]

with typical values \(\lambda_{\text{spec}} = 15\), \(\lambda_{\text{fm}} = 2\), \(\lambda_{\text{adv}} = 1\), \(\lambda_{\text{commit}} = 0.25\). A common practical detail is a warm-up of tens of thousands of steps on reconstruction losses alone before the discriminators are switched on.

NoteA consequence worth remembering

Because Equation 30.10 optimises perceptual and adversarial criteria rather than waveform distance, adversarially-trained codecs can post negative SI-SDR while sounding excellent — DAC reports around \(-1\) to \(-2\) dB. This is not a defect; it means the model reproduces perceptual structure without aligning sample-by-sample. It also means SI-SDR is invalid for comparing codecs with different optimisation objectives, and that mel distance and perceptual scores such as ViSQOL should carry the comparison instead.

30.6 The Codec Landscape

Reading the table chronologically shows two trends running in opposite directions. Fidelity-oriented work (SoundStream → EnCodec → DAC) pushes sample rate and \(N_q\) up. Modelling-oriented work (SpeechTokenizer → Mimi → BigCodec/WavTokenizer) pushes frame rate and \(N_q\) down, accepting fidelity loss in exchange for shorter sequences and a simpler prediction target. These are different objectives, not competing answers to one objective, and papers that benchmark across the two groups on reconstruction metrics alone are comparing things that were not trying to do the same job.

SNAC deserves a note because it breaks an assumption everything else shares. Standard RVQ applies every codebook at the same frame rate; SNAC applies quantisers at different temporal resolutions, so coarse structure is coded sparsely in time and fine structure densely. This is a hierarchy along time as well as depth, and it is the codec behind Orpheus among others.

30.7 Semantic and Acoustic Tokens

Section 31.9.2 introduced the semantic/acoustic split as a modelling decision made outside the codec, using two separate tokenisers. The dominant modern approach folds it inside.

Semantic distillation adds a loss encouraging the first RVQ level’s output to predict the representations of a frozen self-supervised model (Chapter 27) — HuBERT for SpeechTokenizer, WavLM for Mimi. The effect is to make codebook 1 carry phonetic content while leaving speaker identity, prosody, and channel to levels 2 and up. Mimi refines this with split RVQ: rather than distilling into the first level of a single residual chain, it runs a separate single-codebook semantic quantiser in parallel with an acoustic RVQ, so that the semantic objective cannot degrade the acoustic reconstruction path.

The clearest demonstration of what semantic tokens contain is an experiment in the Kyutai explainer, and it is worth reproducing conceptually because it makes an abstract claim concrete. Take a language model trained over full Mimi tokens. Now generate with the semantic tokens held fixed from a reference recording (teacher-forced) while letting the model freely sample all acoustic tokens. Sampling twice yields two outputs that say the same words in completely different voices. The semantic stream determines what is said; the acoustic stream determines how.

That separation is what makes the split useful, for three reasons developed earlier: linguistic coherence over long spans (Section 31.9.2), decoupled data requirements (Section 31.9.2, via SPEAR-TTS), and a natural place to inject speaker identity. A fourth is practical — because the semantic token is \(1/N_q\) of the token budget, its share of the loss falls as \(N_q\) grows, so systems weight it heavily. Moshi uses a semantic loss factor of 100.

TipThe trade-off in one sentence

Lowering \(N_q\) raises the semantic token’s share of the loss and makes the model more linguistic, at the cost of acoustic fidelity — which is why 8-level and 12.5 Hz codecs became the norm for speech LLMs even though 32-level codecs reconstruct better.

30.8 Serialising the Token Grid

Here is the problem this chapter has been deferring. RVQ emits \(N_q\) tokens per frame. A language model emits one token per step. Something has to flatten a two-dimensional grid into a one-dimensional sequence, and the choice is consequential.

The tension is a factorisation question. The exact joint is \(p(\mathbf{c}_t) = \prod_{n} p(c_{t,n} \mid c_{t,<n}, \mathbf{c}_{<t})\). Flattening represents it exactly at maximum sequence-length cost; the parallel pattern drops the within-frame dependency entirely, which is cheap but wrong (the levels of an RVQ frame are strongly dependent by construction, since each codes the residual of the previous); delay patterns and depth transformers are the compromises.

The delay pattern was popularised by MusicGen (Copet et al. 2023), whose figure comparing flattening, parallel, and delayed interleaving is the standard reference diagram for this material. The depth transformer comes from the RQ-Transformer (Lee et al. 2022) and reached speech through Moshi (Défossez et al. 2024), where a 7B temporal backbone predicts one frame’s context and a small depth transformer autoregresses over that frame’s eight levels. Sesame’s CSM uses the same split with per-codebook linear heads, and VALL-E’s AR/NAR pair (Equation 31.11) is yet another point in this space — autoregressive over time for level 1, parallel over depth for the rest.

NoteNo convention has emerged

As of 2026 there is no standard. Every major system picks differently, and the schemes have grown more elaborate rather than converging. This is a reliable signal that the community has not found the right abstraction — and it is a strong argument for single-codebook codecs, which dissolve the problem instead of solving it.

30.9 What Is Actually Inside the Tokens?

The account above is tidy: level 1 is semantic, higher levels are acoustic, identity and prosody live somewhere in the middle. How much of that survives measurement?

A 2025 interpretability study (Sadok et al. 2025) tests it directly across four codecs with contrasting designs — DAC (pure reconstruction), SpeechTokenizer (HuBERT-distilled RVQ), Mimi (split RVQ), and BigCodec (single large VQ) — by tokenising LibriSpeech test-clean and measuring co-occurrence between codec tokens and HuBERT’s 100 discrete units, plus \(t\)-SNE structure and mutual information estimated by a contrastive log-ratio bound.

The conclusion is that content, identity, and pitch are entangled in the quantised latent space even in codecs explicitly designed for disentanglement. The semantic/acoustic story is a real and useful tendency, not a clean factorisation.

This matters practically in both directions. It explains why speaker identity leaks into “semantic” tokens and why voice conversion by naive token surgery does not work cleanly. And it motivates the factorised codecs behind the fourth generation of voice cloning in Section 31.10 — NaturalSpeech 3’s FACodec (Ju et al. 2024) and Spark-TTS’s BiCodec (Wang et al. 2025) — which impose disentanglement through explicit supervision, gradient reversal, and information bottlenecks rather than hoping it emerges from residual structure. Source-disentangled codecs (Bie et al. 2024) pursue the same goal with domain-specific codebooks. The same paper offers a post-hoc route: train a masked encoder-decoder that maps bidirectionally between codec tokens and interpretable attributes (content, pitch, loudness, speaker), which both explains the tokens and enables controlled editing — swapping the speaker attribute performs voice conversion.

The pitch result is the one I would flag for a reader. Pitch is perceptually salient, prosodically essential, and is the attribute a reconstruction objective has least reason to represent explicitly, since it can be reproduced implicitly. If you want prosodic control, do not expect the codec to hand it to you.

30.10 Evaluation

Codecs suffer a version of the evaluation problem already seen in Section 31.12, with a specific twist: reconstruction quality and downstream modelling utility are different objectives, and they can be anti-correlated.

Reconstruction metrics are the mature half. Mel distance (\(L_1\) on log-mel), ViSQOL (Chinen et al. 2020) and PESQ for perceptual quality, STOI for intelligibility, SI-SDR for waveform alignment (with the caveat above that it is meaningless across differing objectives), and MUSHRA or P.808 listening tests. Codebook entropy and utilisation measure how much of the nominal bitrate is actually used.

Downstream utility is what a speech LLM builder needs and is far less settled. Two benchmarks address it. Codec-SUPERB (Wu et al. 2024) evaluates codecs by how well downstream models perform on resynthesised audio across recognition, speaker, and content tasks. DASB (Mousavi et al. 2024) ranks discrete audio tokens across discriminative and generative tasks, and reports the result this section is built around: semantic tokens generally outperform compression-optimised acoustic tokens on downstream tasks, while continuous representations still outperform both.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. A codec that wins on ViSQOL may lose as a tokeniser, so reconstruction leaderboards do not rank tokenisers. And the finding from the tonal study (Mondal and Gupta 2026) that higher-fidelity codecs yield embeddings correlating somewhat better with human judgement — while both remain below purpose-built contrastive audio encoders — is a useful reminder that “the codec’s latent space” and “a good audio representation” are related but not the same object.

Toolkits: ESPnet-Codec (Shi et al. 2024) provides training and evaluation across codecs with a unified metric suite, and FunCodec (Du et al. 2023) offers reproducible multi-rate models.

30.11 Do We Need Discrete Tokens at All?

The entire chapter rests on a premise worth interrogating, especially since Section 31.9.5 already showed it is contestable in synthesis.

The case for discreteness is architectural rather than representational. Discrete tokens let you reuse the language-modelling stack unchanged: categorical cross-entropy, sampling with temperature and nucleus filtering, KV caching, pretrained text-LLM weights, and preference optimisation. That is a large amount of borrowed infrastructure, and it is the entire reason the paradigm exists.

The case against is that quantisation destroys information for no modelling reason. DASB’s finding that continuous representations beat discrete tokens on downstream tasks is direct evidence. MELLE (Section 31.9.5) generates continuous mel frames autoregressively and is more robust than its discrete-token counterpart. And a growing line of work generates continuous audio latents with a small diffusion or flow-matching head per step, keeping autoregression over time while discarding quantisation.

The honest position is that discreteness is an engineering convenience whose representational cost is real and whose necessity is unproven. It is the same open question raised at the end of Section 27.9, arriving from the generative side.

30.12 Open Problems

  1. The fidelity/modellability trade-off is unquantified. Everyone knows lower frame rates make sequences easier to model and reconstructions worse. Nobody can predict where the optimum sits for a given downstream model and compute budget, and there is no theory relating the two.
  2. Disentanglement remains aspirational. As Section 30.9 shows, even codecs built for it leak. Whether clean factorisation into content, speaker, prosody, and channel is achievable without heavy explicit supervision is open.
  3. Serialisation has no principled answer. Table 30.3 is a list of engineering compromises, not a solution. Single-codebook codecs sidestep it, which may be the real lesson.
  4. Pitch and prosody are under-represented. A reconstruction objective has no reason to encode them explicitly, and measurement confirms it does not.
  5. Content-dependent failure is under-studied. Tonal material is one documented case (Mondal and Gupta 2026); the general question of which signal classes a codec systematically mishandles, and whether that is a data problem or a capacity problem, is largely unexplored. Aggregate metrics hide it by construction.
  6. Evaluation for tokenisers, not compressors. Codec-SUPERB and DASB are starts, but there is no accepted way to predict how a codec will behave inside a speech LLM without training one.
  7. Streaming and latency constraints. Causality, low algorithmic delay, and cheap decoding are required for duplex dialogue and constrain everything else; Mimi’s sub-10 ms CPU decode is the target to beat.
  8. Non-speech audio and general audio. Most speech codecs degrade on music and environmental sound. Whether one tokeniser should serve all audio, or whether domain-specific codecs are the right answer, is unsettled.

30.13 Summary

A neural audio codec is an encoder, a quantiser, and a decoder, trained end-to-end with spectral, adversarial, feature-matching, and commitment losses. It exists because language models need discrete tokens at a manageable rate: sample-level modelling puts less than one word inside a typical context window, which is a failure of coherence no amount of training repairs.

The technical core is vector quantisation made trainable. The \(\arg\min\) lookup has no gradient, so the straight-through estimator copies the gradient past it; the commitment loss keeps that approximation honest by pulling latents toward their codes; EMA updates and dead-code reinitialisation keep the codebook alive. Residual quantisation then buys exponential vocabulary from linear storage by quantising each stage’s error, which incidentally orders the levels by importance and makes bitrate truncatable — and which produces the two-dimensional token grid that every downstream language model must serialise, with no consensus on how.

Codebook collapse is the recurring adversary, and the progression of fixes — diversity losses, EMA and dead-code reinitialisation, factorised and normalised lookup, and finally finite scalar quantisation, which removes the codebook entirely — is the same movement toward structural rather than corrective solutions seen in Section 27.4.1.

Two ideas deserve to be carried forward. The semantic/acoustic split, now usually folded inside the codec by distilling a self-supervised model into the first level, is what makes speech language modelling a linguistic rather than an acoustic act; holding semantic tokens fixed while resampling the rest yields the same words in a different voice, which is the cleanest demonstration available of what these representations contain. And the empirical caveat to that story: measurement shows content, identity, and pitch remain entangled even in codecs designed to separate them, with pitch the least represented of all.

Chapter 32 takes the token grid as an interface and asks what happens when a language model is trained to read and write it — and why, so far, doing so makes the language model worse at thinking.

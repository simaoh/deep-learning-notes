27  Speech Representation Learning and Foundation Models

The previous chapter ended with an economic problem. End-to-end ASR works, but it wants thousands of hours of transcribed audio per language, and transcription is expensive, slow, and requires literate speakers of the target language. There are roughly 7,000 living languages. Fewer than a hundred have the labelled corpora that Section 28.6 assumes.

Unlabelled speech, by contrast, is nearly free. Radio archives, podcasts, parliamentary recordings, and video platforms supply orders of magnitude more audio than anyone has ever transcribed. Self-supervised learning is the attempt to convert that abundance into a usable prior: pre-train an encoder on raw audio with an objective that requires no transcripts, then fine-tune it on whatever small labelled set exists.

This chapter is about how that is done, what the resulting representations actually contain, and how they are evaluated. It is shorter than the last chapter because the conceptual content is smaller — essentially one idea with three implementations — but its reach is larger. The encoders described here are the front-end of nearly every system in the rest of this book: the speaker embeddings of Section 29.1.1, the semantic tokens of Section 30.7, the emotion and audio-captioning models of Chapter 33, and the audio inputs of speech language models all begin with a network trained by one of these objectives.

NoteThe one-sentence summary

The recipe that won is masked prediction of discrete units, and the three families in this chapter are three different answers to the question where do the discrete targets come from?

27.1 Why Self-Supervision Is Hard for Speech

Self-supervision means inventing a pretext task: a supervised problem whose labels are derived from the input itself. In text this is easy, because language arrives pre-discretised. BERT masks a token and asks which of 30,000 vocabulary items it was; the answer is a well-defined categorical variable, cross-entropy applies, and the model is forced to learn syntax and semantics to succeed.

Speech has no vocabulary. The signal is continuous, high-rate, and — this is the crux — locally very predictable for uninteresting reasons. Given the waveform up to time \(t\), the sample at \(t+1\) is almost determined by the local waveform’s smoothness. A model trained to regress the next sample, or the next spectrogram frame, under an \(L_2\) loss will spend nearly all of its capacity on low-level detail: signal continuity, channel colouration, background noise level, speaker timbre. None of that is what a downstream task needs.

Three design problems follow, and every model in this chapter is positioned by how it answers them.

  1. What is the prediction target? Regressing the raw signal invites the failure above. The alternative is to predict something quantised — to force the model to commit to categorical, phone-like distinctions rather than hedging with a conditional mean. This is the central insight of the field.
  2. Where does the quantisation come from? There is no ground-truth phone inventory. The targets must be discovered, and the discovery is circular: good units require a good model, and a good model requires good units.
  3. What is masked, and at what granularity? Speech is redundant over short windows. Masking a single 20 ms frame is trivially solvable by interpolation, so useful pretext tasks mask spans long enough that local smoothness is not enough.

27.2 Contrastive Objectives

27.2.1 Contrastive Predictive Coding

CPC (Oord et al. 2018) is the ancestor. Its move is to abandon reconstruction entirely: rather than predicting the future signal, predict the future latent, and score the prediction by discrimination rather than regression.

An encoder \(f\) maps the input to latents \(\mathbf{z}_t = f(\mathbf{x}_t)\), and an autoregressive network \(g\) summarises the past into a context \(\mathbf{c}_t = g(\mathbf{z}_{\leq t})\). For each offset \(k\), a bilinear scorer must pick the true future latent \(\mathbf{z}_{t+k}\) out of a set \(\mathcal{Z}\) containing one positive and \(N-1\) negatives sampled from elsewhere:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{\exp\!\big(\mathbf{z}_{t+k}^{\top} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{c}_t\big)} {\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{z}} \in \mathcal{Z}} \exp\!\big(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^{\top} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{c}_t\big)}\right] \tag{27.1}\]

Two properties make this the right shape for speech. First, it is a variational lower bound on mutual information: minimising Equation 27.1 maximises a bound \(I(\mathbf{z}_{t+k}; \mathbf{c}_t) \geq \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}}\), so the context is pushed to retain exactly the information that is predictive of the future and free to discard everything else. Second, discrimination against negatives sidesteps the conditional-mean problem: there is no averaging, because the model never has to produce a signal, only to rank candidates.

wav2vec (Schneider et al. 2019) applied this to ASR directly — a fully convolutional encoder and context network trained with a CPC-style loss on unlabelled audio, whose output replaces log-Mel features as the input to an acoustic model. It was the first clear demonstration that self-supervised pre-training reduces WER on standard benchmarks, which converted SSL from a representation-learning curiosity into a speech method.

vq-wav2vec (Baevski, Schneider, et al. 2020) is the pivotal intermediate step and the best teaching device in this section. It inserts a vector quantiser so that audio becomes a sequence of discrete tokens, which can then be fed to an off-the-shelf BERT. Two consequences follow, one immediate and one that took three years to pay off: the two-stage pipeline worked well, and — more importantly — it established that speech could be treated as a sequence of symbols drawn from a learned vocabulary. Every discrete-token model in this book, including the neural audio codecs and the speech language models, descends from that framing.

27.2.2 wav2vec 2.0

wav2vec 2.0 (Baevski, Zhou, et al. 2020) collapses vq-wav2vec’s two stages into one jointly-trained model and is, by influence, the most important paper in this chapter.

The architecture has three parts. A feature encoder — a seven-layer temporal CNN with 20 ms stride — maps the raw waveform to latents \(\mathbf{z}_t\). A context network, a Transformer, maps the (partially masked) latent sequence to contextualised representations \(\mathbf{c}_t\). And a quantiser maps each unmasked latent to a discrete code \(\mathbf{q}_t\), which serves as the prediction target.

Spans are masked in the latent sequence: a proportion of time steps are sampled as start indices and the following \(M \approx 10\) steps (200 ms) are replaced with a learned mask embedding. The model must then identify the true quantised latent at each masked position, against distractors sampled from other masked positions in the same utterance:

\[ \mathcal{L}_m = -\log \frac{\exp\!\big(\text{sim}(\mathbf{c}_t, \mathbf{q}_t)/\kappa\big)} {\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}} \sim \mathbf{Q}_t} \exp\!\big(\text{sim}(\mathbf{c}_t, \tilde{\mathbf{q}})/\kappa\big)} \tag{27.2}\]

with \(\text{sim}\) the cosine similarity and \(\kappa\) a temperature.

Product quantisation and the Gumbel trick. The quantiser uses \(G\) codebooks of \(V\) entries each; a latent is mapped to one entry per codebook and the \(G\) selections are concatenated and projected, giving \(V^G\) effective targets from only \(GV\) parameters (in the original, \(G = 2\), \(V = 320\), hence 102,400 possible targets). Selection is a discrete \(\arg\max\), which has no gradient, so training uses the Gumbel-softmax with a straight-through estimator: the forward pass takes the hard \(\arg\max\), the backward pass uses

\[ p_{g,v} = \frac{\exp\big((l_{g,v} + n_v)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{k=1}^{V} \exp\big((l_{g,k} + n_k)/\tau\big)}, \qquad n_v = -\log(-\log u_v), \; u_v \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) \tag{27.3}\]

with the temperature \(\tau\) annealed from 2 to 0.5 over training, moving the relaxation gradually toward the hard discrete behaviour it approximates.

Codebook collapse and the diversity loss. Left alone, a learned quantiser degenerates: a handful of entries absorb all the probability mass, the effective vocabulary shrinks, and the contrastive task becomes trivial because the distractors are usually the same code as the positive. The fix is an auxiliary term that maximises the entropy of average codebook usage across a batch,

\[ \mathcal{L}_d = \frac{1}{GV}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\Big(-H(\bar{p}_g)\Big) = \frac{1}{GV}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{v=1}^{V} \bar{p}_{g,v} \log \bar{p}_{g,v} \tag{27.4}\]

where \(\bar{p}_{g,v}\) is the mean softmax probability of entry \(v\) in codebook \(g\) over the batch. The total objective is \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_m + \alpha \mathcal{L}_d\). Codebook collapse recurs throughout this book — it is the same failure that afflicts the neural audio codecs of Section 30.4 — and Equation 27.4 is the first of several answers to it.

Why it mattered. The headline result reframed the economics of ASR: competitive word error rates from ten minutes of labelled speech, and 4.8/8.2% on LibriSpeech test-clean/test-other using only that, after pre-training on the 53k unlabelled hours of Libri-Light (Kahn et al. 2020). Fine-tuning is simply a CTC head (Section 28.6.1) on top of the context network. For the thousands of languages with no transcribed corpora, this was the unlock, and it is the reason the multilingual programme of Section 27.7 became thinkable.

27.3 Masked Prediction with Learned Targets

27.3.1 HuBERT

wav2vec 2.0 trains the quantiser and the context model against each other, which works but is delicate — the target distribution moves while it is being predicted. HuBERT (Hsu et al. 2021) sidesteps the instability with a deliberately low-tech alternative: compute the targets offline, then treat the problem as ordinary masked classification.

The procedure is a loop:

  1. Run \(k\)-means on 39-dimensional MFCCs of the training audio. The resulting cluster assignments \(z_t \in \{1, \ldots, C\}\) are noisy pseudo-labels — but they are categorical, which is all the objective needs.
  2. Mask spans of the input and train a Transformer to predict the cluster label at masked positions, with a cross-entropy over a learned code embedding \(\mathbf{e}_c\):

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t \in M} \log p(z_t \mid \tilde{\mathbf{X}}, t), \qquad p(c \mid \tilde{\mathbf{X}}, t) = \frac{\exp\big(\text{sim}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{o}_t, \mathbf{e}_c)/\tau\big)} {\sum_{c'=1}^{C} \exp\big(\text{sim}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{o}_t, \mathbf{e}_{c'})/\tau\big)} \tag{27.5}\]

where \(\mathbf{o}_t\) is the Transformer output, \(\mathbf{A}\) a projection, and \(M\) the masked index set. 3. Re-run \(k\)-means, now on the hidden representations of the model just trained, to obtain better labels. Return to step 2.

Two details carry most of the weight. First, the loss is computed only at masked positions. Scoring unmasked frames as well lets the model solve the task by copying the local input through, which is exactly the trivial solution of Section 27.1; restricting to masked spans forces it to use context. This single choice is worth a large accuracy difference and is the clearest demonstration in the chapter that what you do not train on matters as much as what you do.

Second, the iterative refinement genuinely bootstraps. MFCC clusters are barely better than chance at separating phones. But a model trained to predict them must learn contextual structure to do so, and its hidden layers therefore organise phonetically; clustering those yields substantially cleaner units, and the next iteration is better again. Measured phone purity climbs at each round. The circularity identified in Section 27.1 is resolved not by solving it but by iterating through it — the same trick as the forced-alignment loop of the classical ASR pipeline, and worth pointing out as such.

HuBERT’s discrete units became the field’s default “semantic tokens”, and that is arguably its largest downstream consequence. When Section 30.7 describes a codec whose first residual codebook is distilled to match WavLM features, or a speech language model operating over discrete units, the lineage runs through Equation 27.5.

27.3.2 WavLM

WavLM (Chen et al. 2022) keeps HuBERT’s objective and changes the data. During pre-training, utterances are augmented by mixing in noise and — crucially — overlapping speech from other utterances, while the prediction target remains the units of the original clean primary speaker. The masked-prediction task therefore becomes a masked denoising task: the model must reconstruct what the target speaker said while ignoring an interfering one.

The consequences are exactly what that objective would predict. WavLM is the strongest general-purpose speech encoder of its generation on tasks that require holding onto speaker identity under adverse conditions: speaker verification, diarization (Section 29.1.1), and separation. Its lesson generalises well beyond speech — robustness is a property you train for, not a property you bolt on — and it explains why WavLM, rather than a newer or larger model, is still the standard backbone for speaker similarity scoring, including the speaker similarity metric used to evaluate zero-shot speech synthesis (Section 31.12.2).

27.3.3 data2vec

data2vec (Baevski et al. 2022) asks whether the targets need to be discrete at all. It replaces them with contextualised latent targets produced by an exponential-moving-average teacher — the same network, at a lagged parameter average:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{t \in M} \big\| f_\theta(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})_t - \mathbf{y}_t \big\|^2, \qquad \mathbf{y}_t = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{l = L-K+1}^{L} \hat{\mathbf{a}}_t^{(l)}, \qquad \Delta \leftarrow \tau\Delta + (1-\tau)\theta \tag{27.6}\]

where \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_t^{(l)}\) are normalised activations of the teacher’s top \(K\) layers and \(\Delta\) are the teacher parameters. Because the teacher sees the unmasked input, its representation at a masked position is contextual rather than local, which avoids the trivial-solution problem without needing quantisation.

Its significance is partly methodological: the identical algorithm, with only the input encoder changed, works for speech, vision, and text. It is the modality-agnostic pole of the design space, and a useful counterweight to the claim that discretisation is essential.

27.4 Random-Projection Quantisation

The last family is the simplest and, measured by citations alone, the most underrated in this chapter.

BEST-RQ (Chiu et al. 2022) observes that if the point of quantisation is merely to produce consistent categorical targets, the quantiser does not have to be learned, or even good. It uses a projection matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) and a codebook \(\mathbf{C} = [\mathbf{c}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{c}_V]\) that are randomly initialised and then frozen forever:

\[ y_t = \arg\min_{v \in \{1,\ldots,V\}} \big\| \, \text{norm}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_t) - \mathbf{c}_v \, \big\|_2 \tag{27.7}\]

with training a plain cross-entropy on masked positions against these labels. There is no codebook learning, no diversity loss, no clustering loop, no EMA teacher, and no moving target.

That this works at all is the interesting part, and the reason is worth stating. A random projection is, with high probability, approximately distance-preserving (the Johnson–Lindenstrauss property), so acoustically similar frames land near each other in the projected space and therefore receive the same label. The labels are not phonetic, but they are consistent: the same sound gets the same symbol. Consistency, it turns out, is the only property the masked-prediction objective actually requires — the phonetic organisation is something the model builds for itself in order to predict those labels from context.

Why it is under-cited relative to its influence. BEST-RQ was adopted primarily inside industry rather than reproduced in academia. It is the pre-training objective of Google’s USM (Zhang et al. 2023) and hence of production ASR across 100+ languages, and it has propagated into subsequent large-scale audio systems. Its citation count sits an order of magnitude below wav2vec 2.0’s while its deployed footprint is comparable. It belongs in any teaching treatment as the third canonical objective, alongside contrastive learning and offline-cluster masked prediction.

TipReading citation counts

BEST-RQ is a good calibration case for the impact heuristics used elsewhere in this book. Papers whose contribution ships inside a product rather than a checkpoint are systematically under-cited, because the citations accrue to the system, not the method. Treat a low count on an industrial-lab paper as weak evidence, not as a verdict.

27.4.1 Comparing the Families

The trajectory across the table is toward simplicity: each family removes machinery from the previous one while matching or beating it. That is usually a sign that the field has identified which part of the original method was doing the work — here, masked prediction against consistent categorical targets — and is discarding the scaffolding that surrounded it.

w2v-BERT (Chung et al. 2021) is the notable hybrid, combining a contrastive module (whose job is to learn the discrete inventory) with a masked-LM module (whose job is to model context over it) in a single end-to-end model. It is the speech encoder of SeamlessM4T and, by inheritance, of a considerable amount of multilingual speech infrastructure.

27.5 What Do These Models Actually Learn?

This section contains the single most useful empirical fact in the chapter, and it is the one that connects it to every other chapter in the book.

Self-supervised speech encoders are not uniform. Probing each Transformer layer with a light classifier reveals a consistent layer-wise division of labour (Pasad et al. 2021):

The last row is the counter-intuitive one and deserves an explanation. In large wav2vec 2.0 models the topmost layers move measurably back toward the local, quantised input features they were trained to predict, producing an autoencoder-like curve in which representational quality for phonetic and lexical content peaks in the middle of the network and then declines. The practical implication is direct: the final layer is usually the wrong choice, which is the opposite of the convention inherited from vision and text models.

Two further consequences run through this book:

  • ASR and speaker tasks want opposite ends of the same network. Recognition benefits from suppressing speaker identity, so it draws on upper-middle layers; diarization and verification need speaker identity preserved, so they draw on the lowest layers (Section 29.1.1). One encoder serves both only because it happens to keep the two kinds of information at different depths.
  • The standard remedy is to learn the mixture. Rather than committing to a layer, downstream heads take a weighted sum over all \(L{+}1\) layer outputs with learnable weights,

\[ \mathbf{h}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{L} w_l \, \mathbf{h}_t^{(l)}, \qquad \mathbf{w} = \operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) \tag{27.8}\]

where only \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathbb{R}^{L+1}\) is trained alongside the head. Inspecting the learned \(\mathbf{w}\) afterwards is itself a diagnostic: the weight profile tells you where in the encoder your task’s information lives.

27.6 Evaluation: SUPERB and Its Discontents

Comparing self-supervised encoders is harder than comparing ASR systems, because an encoder is not a system. Fine-tune it aggressively enough and any sufficiently large model looks good, which measures the fine-tuning budget rather than the representation.

SUPERB (Yang et al. 2021) resolved this with a protocol as important as its leaderboard: freeze the encoder, train only a lightweight task-specific head, and use the learnable layer weighting of Equation 27.8. That grouping is itself a claim worth noticing: it asserts that a good speech representation must serve speaker, content, semantic, and paralinguistic purposes simultaneously — which is precisely what Section 27.5 says a single encoder can only do by stratifying them across depth.

ML-SUPERB (Shi et al. 2023) extends the protocol to 143 languages with ASR and language identification, and remains the main multilingual representation benchmark.

Known weaknesses, which should be stated whenever SUPERB numbers are quoted:

  • The frozen-encoder, light-head protocol does not necessarily predict performance under full fine-tuning, and almost certainly does not predict performance when the encoder is attached to an LLM decoder (Section 28.8.2) — the dominant deployment pattern today.
  • Scores are near-saturated on several tasks, so ranking differences are within noise.
  • The head architecture and optimisation budget, though standardised, still advantage some representation geometries over others.

27.7 Multilingual Scaling

Self-supervision’s most consequential application is the long tail of languages. Because pre-training needs no transcripts, a single model can absorb audio from hundreds of languages at once and transfer to any of them.

The central empirical finding is positive cross-lingual transfer: a model pre-trained on many languages outperforms a monolingual model on the same low-resource target, because phonetic and articulatory structure is largely shared even when lexicons are not. The effect is strongest exactly where it is needed most, for languages with a few hours of data or fewer.

It is bounded, however, by the curse of multilinguality: at fixed capacity, adding languages eventually degrades per-language quality, as the model trades specialisation for coverage. This is the representation-learning counterpart of the coverage tax measured on ASR leaderboards (Section 28.11.6), and it is why the largest multilingual models are also the largest models.

WarningA data caveat worth teaching

MMS reached 1,000+ languages by using recordings of religious texts — chiefly New Testament translations — because that is the only broadly parallel multilingual speech corpus in existence. It is a genuinely clever piece of data sourcing, and it also means the training distribution is a single register (read, formal, liturgical), typically one or few speakers per language, with a lexicon skewed toward a specific domain. Reported coverage of a language should not be read as usable ASR for that language’s everyday speech.

27.8 Foundation Encoders in Practice

27.8.1 Whisper as a Feature Extractor

A pattern worth flagging because it now dominates deployment: the encoder from a large supervised model is often used exactly as a self-supervised one would be. Whisper’s encoder (Section 28.8.1), trained on 680k hours of weakly-labelled data, produces representations that transfer well and are the input to a large share of current audio language models, usually via a projector into an LLM’s embedding space.

The comparison to genuinely self-supervised encoders is instructive rather than one-sided. Whisper’s encoder is trained toward transcription, so it actively suppresses information that is nuisance for that task — speaker identity, emotion, background acoustics. This makes it an excellent front-end for content-oriented tasks and a measurably poorer one for paralinguistic tasks, a weakness that resurfaces in Section 32.5 as one mechanism behind audio language models’ difficulty with emotion and speaker attributes. WavLM remains the better choice when the downstream task is about who is speaking or how rather than what.

27.8.2 Discrete Units as an Interface

Running \(k\)-means over the middle layers of HuBERT or WavLM and keeping the cluster indices yields a sequence of discrete symbols at roughly 50 Hz. These are the semantic tokens referred to throughout the generative chapters of this book. They capture phonetic and linguistic content while discarding speaker identity and acoustic detail — which is to say, they are not invertible to audio, and that is precisely why they are paired with acoustic tokens from a neural codec in later architectures.

The interface matters more than any particular model: once speech is a sequence of integers from a finite vocabulary, the entire language-modelling toolkit applies unchanged. That observation is what the codec and speech-language-model chapters are built on, and it originates here.

27.8.3 Efficiency

Full-size encoders are expensive: wav2vec 2.0 Large and WavLM Large are around 300M parameters and were pre-trained on hundreds of GPU-days. Two responses are standard. Distillation compresses a large encoder into a much smaller student that retains most of the SUPERB performance — DistilHuBERT (Chang et al. 2022) predicts multiple teacher layers from a shared shallow student, a design that follows directly from Section 27.5, since different layers carry different information and must be preserved separately. Partial fine-tuning freezes the encoder and trains only adapters or the layer weights of Equation 27.8, which is often within noise of full fine-tuning at a fraction of the cost.

27.8.4 Toolkits

Extracting layer-wise features is a few lines:

import torch
from transformers import AutoModel, AutoFeatureExtractor

name = "microsoft/wavlm-large"          # strongest general-purpose encoder
fe = AutoFeatureExtractor.from_pretrained(name)
model = AutoModel.from_pretrained(name, output_hidden_states=True).eval()

inputs = fe(waveform, sampling_rate=16_000, return_tensors="pt")
with torch.no_grad():
    hidden = model(**inputs).hidden_states   # tuple of (L+1) tensors

# Weighted layer sum; alpha is learned jointly with the downstream head
alpha = torch.zeros(len(hidden), requires_grad=True)
features = torch.einsum("l,lbtd->btd", alpha.softmax(0), torch.stack(hidden))

27.9 Open Problems

  1. What the units mean. Discovered units correlate with phones but are not phones: they are context-dependent, split by speaker and channel, and vary in granularity with \(k\). There is no principled account of what inventory a given objective and cluster count will produce, and no way to choose \(k\) other than by downstream trial.
  2. Scaling laws are poorly characterised. For text, the relationship between compute, data, parameters, and loss is well mapped. For speech self-supervision it is not, and the pre-training loss is a weak predictor of downstream quality — a model with lower masked-prediction loss is not reliably a better encoder.
  3. The low-resource tail is still poor. Coverage claims of 1,000+ languages are real, but quality at the tail is weak, the training data is often a single narrow register, and evaluation data barely exists — which means the tail is simultaneously the least good and the least measurable.
  4. Evaluation has outgrown SUPERB. The frozen-encoder protocol was the right answer for 2021 and does not predict behaviour under the dominant current pattern of attaching an encoder to an LLM decoder. There is no widely accepted successor.
  5. Continuous or discrete? This chapter’s dominant recipe quantises the target, and downstream generative work quantises the representation. But data2vec works without quantisation, continuous features still beat discrete tokens on most discriminative downstream tasks, and the generative side has an active continuous-latent counter-movement. The question is genuinely open, and it recurs in Section 30.11.
  6. Robustness and fairness. Encoders inherit the demographic and acoustic composition of their unlabelled pre-training data. Because that data is unlabelled, its composition is typically unknown, which makes the resulting biases harder to audit than the labelled-data disparities of Section 28.11.7 rather than easier.
  7. Paralinguistics are under-served. Objectives reward predicting what was said. Prosody, emotion, and speaking style are largely incidental to that, and the resulting representational weakness propagates into every downstream paralinguistic task in this book.

27.10 Summary

Self-supervised pre-training is the field’s answer to the fact that unlabelled speech outnumbers transcribed speech by orders of magnitude. The difficulty is that speech is continuous and locally predictable, so a naive reconstruction objective learns signal smoothness rather than linguistic structure. The resolution the field converged on is masked prediction against consistent discrete targets, and the three canonical objectives differ only in where those targets come from: jointly-learned vector quantisation trained contrastively (wav2vec 2.0), offline \(k\)-means labels refined by iterating the model against its own representations (HuBERT, plus WavLM’s denoising augmentation), and a frozen random-projection codebook that is never learned at all (BEST-RQ). The progression is toward removing machinery, which is the usual signature of a field discovering which component was actually load-bearing.

The practical payoff is threefold. Usable ASR from minutes rather than thousands of hours of labelled audio; a single encoder that transfers across hundreds of languages through shared phonetic structure; and a general-purpose front-end that serves speaker, content, and semantic tasks at once — though only because, as Section 27.5 shows, it stratifies those kinds of information across depth, which is why the final layer is usually the wrong one to use and why ASR and diarization draw from opposite ends of the same network.

Two threads leave this chapter for elsewhere in the book. Discrete units obtained by clustering these encoders are the semantic tokens that the codec and speech-language-model chapters depend on, and the pattern of projecting a frozen encoder into a language model’s embedding space — whether that encoder is WavLM or Whisper’s — is the architecture of essentially every audio LLM.

The next chapter turns to the task that these representations were most immediately useful for beyond recognition: determining not what was said, but who said it, and when.

31  Speech Synthesis

Text-to-Speech (TTS) maps a written sentence to a spoken waveform. Given a token sequence \(\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_U)\) the task is to produce \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_T) \in \mathbb{R}^{T}\), a waveform that a listener would accept as a natural rendering of \(\mathbf{y}\).

It is tempting to call this “ASR in reverse”, and the sequence-length arithmetic of Chapter 28 does invert cleanly: a 25-token sentence becomes 160,000 samples rather than the other way round. But the inversion is not symmetric in the way that matters, and the asymmetry is the subject of this chapter.

Recognition is many-to-one. Infinitely many waveforms — different speakers, accents, rooms, microphones, speaking rates — map to the same transcript. The target is essentially unique, so “correct” is well defined, an error rate can be computed by string edit distance, and supervised learning applies without philosophical difficulty.

Synthesis is one-to-many. A single sentence admits infinitely many valid renderings, differing in prosody, timing, emphasis, breathing, voice quality, and recording channel. There is no unique correct output. The recorded audio that accompanies a sentence in a training corpus is one sample from \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{y})\), not the target.

\[ \mathbf{x} \sim p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{y}), \qquad p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{y}) \text{ high-entropy and multi-modal} \tag{31.1}\]

Almost everything peculiar about TTS follows from Equation 31.1. It is why regression to the recorded waveform is the wrong objective; why the field needed generative models — adversarial, variational, diffusion, flow-matching, autoregressive — rather than discriminative ones; why controllability is a first-class research problem rather than a feature request; and why evaluation is itself a research problem in TTS to a degree that has no parallel in recognition. Where Chapter 28 closed with a section on evaluation being more contested than the modelling, this chapter has to treat that contest as part of the science.

31.1 Notation and Key Concepts

31.1.1 The Classical Decomposition

Every TTS system, neural or not, performs three transformations. They may be separate modules, jointly trained stages, or collapsed into one network, but it is worth naming them because the vocabulary persists even where the module boundaries do not.

  1. Text front-end (analysis). Normalise the raw text — numbers, dates, currency, abbreviations, URLs — and convert it to a linguistic representation, classically phonemes via grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversion with stress and phrase boundaries.
  2. Acoustic model. Map the linguistic sequence to an intermediate acoustic representation at a low frame rate, classically a mel spectrogram.
  3. Vocoder (synthesis). Convert the acoustic representation into a waveform.
NoteA word that changed meaning

A vocoder (voice coder) was originally a low-bitrate speech codec: analyse the signal into excitation and spectral envelope, transmit those, resynthesise. In neural TTS the word now means only “the module that turns acoustic features into a waveform” — a decoder, not a codec. Confusingly, Section 31.9 then reintroduces genuine codecs under their original meaning. Both usages are standard; context disambiguates.

31.1.2 Why Mel Spectrograms

The mel spectrogram became the interface between stages 2 and 3 for three reasons worth stating explicitly, because two of them later turned into problems.

It is compact: 80 dimensions at 86 Hz instead of 24,000 samples per second, a reduction of roughly \(350\times\), which puts the hard sequence-modelling problem at a tractable rate. It is perceptually motivated: mel-spaced filters and log compression approximate cochlear frequency resolution and loudness perception (Equation 28.2), so squared error in this domain is at least loosely related to audibility. And it discards phase, which is a feature for the predictor — phase wraps, is perceptually strange, and is genuinely hard to regress — while being a liability for the reconstructor, which must hallucinate it back. Griffin-Lim phase estimation, used by the earliest neural systems, is the audible demonstration: its characteristic smeared, metallic artefact is precisely the cost of not having phase.

The deeper problem is that mel is lossy and not learned. It is fixed by hand rather than optimised jointly with either the model that predicts it or the model that consumes it, and the vocoder is trained on ground-truth mels but deployed on predicted ones. Much of the field’s recent movement — toward codec tokens, VAE latents, and waveform latents — is an attempt to replace this hand-designed interface with a learned one.

31.2 The One-to-Many Problem

This section plays the role that Section 28.2 plays in the ASR chapter: it states the difficulty from which the architectures follow.

31.2.1 Why Regression Fails

Suppose we train an acoustic model by minimising squared error against the recorded mel spectrogram. The minimiser of the expected loss is, pointwise, the conditional mean:

\[ f^{\star} = \arg\min_{f} \; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{M}} \big\| \mathbf{M} - f(\mathbf{y}) \big\|_2^2 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad f^{\star}(\mathbf{y}) = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{y}\big] \tag{31.2}\]

and under an \(L_1\) loss, the conditional median. Both are the correct answers to the question being asked, and both are useless. Squared error is the negative log-likelihood of an isotropic Gaussian and \(L_1\) that of a Laplacian; both are unimodal and factorised across dimensions, so fitting them asserts that the mel bins are conditionally independent and that \(p(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{y})\) has a single mode. Neither assumption is close to true. Mel bins are strongly correlated in time and frequency, and the conditional is genuinely multi-modal: a syllable may plausibly be realised with rising or falling pitch, and the mean of those two contours is a third contour that no speaker would produce.

The perceptual signature is over-smoothing: blurred harmonic structure, attenuated high frequencies, washed-out formant transitions. Speech that sounds muffled, buzzy, and lifeless. The vocoder then compounds the damage, because it is asked to invert a smeared spectrogram unlike anything in its training set.

Over-smoothing is not a neural-network problem. It afflicted HMM-based synthesis for the same reason, in the same way, a decade earlier — and recognising the continuity is the fastest route into the field’s history.

31.2.2 Three Strategies

There are exactly three coherent responses to Equation 31.2, and every system built since 2019 is one of them or a mixture (Ren et al. 2022).

  1. Reduce the entropy of the conditional by conditioning on more. If \(p(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{y})\) is high-entropy, condition additionally on the variables that explain the variation — duration, pitch, energy, speaker, style, or a reference recording — so that \(p(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{y}, \text{extras})\) is nearly unimodal and a simple loss becomes a less-wrong model of it. Predict the extras separately. This is FastSpeech 2’s variance adaptor, and, in a generalised form, every prompt-based zero-shot system.
  2. Use a model class that can represent multi-modality. Categorical softmaxes, GANs, VAEs, normalising flows, diffusion, flow matching, and discrete-token language models all can. This is WaveNet, HiFi-GAN, VITS, Grad-TTS, Voicebox, and VALL-E.
  3. Model the unexplained residual as an explicit latent. Push whatever text does not determine into a latent variable inferred at training time and sampled at inference. This is Parallel Tacotron’s VAE and VITS’s posterior.

A useful way to hold the generative families together: each is a different answer to where the stochasticity lives. In a GAN it is in the input noise, judged by a critic. In diffusion and flow matching it is in a sampled trajectory. In a VAE it is in a latent. In a discrete LM it is in the per-step categorical sample. Once you see that, the apparent zoo of architectures becomes a small set of choices.

31.3 Historical Overview

31.4 Before Deep Learning

Two paradigms competed, and the trade-off between them is still recognisable in modern systems.

Unit selection concatenates fragments of recorded speech, chosen from a large single-speaker database by minimising a target cost (how well each unit matches the desired context) plus a join cost (how smoothly consecutive units splice). Because the output is recorded speech, local segmental quality can be excellent. But the voice cannot be changed without re-recording, prosody is limited to what the database contains, and joins produce audible glitches.

Statistical parametric synthesis (Zen et al. 2009), in practice HMM-based synthesis (HTS), models the trajectory of acoustic parameters — mel-cepstra, log \(F_0\), band aperiodicity — with context-dependent HMMs and decision-tree state tying, then reconstructs the waveform with a source-filter vocoder such as STRAIGHT or WORLD (Morise et al. 2016). This bought flexibility: speaker adaptation, style control, and a footprint measured in megabytes rather than gigabytes. It cost quality, and it is where over-smoothing was first diagnosed.

The 2009 review (Zen et al. 2009) attributes the quality gap to exactly three causes, and the sequence in which deep learning attacked them organises the rest of this chapter:

Diagnosed in 2009, resolved in that order over the following decade. Few subfields have a roadmap laid out so cleanly in advance.

31.5 Neural Vocoders

31.5.1 WaveNet and the Sequential Bottleneck

WaveNet (Oord et al. 2016) models the waveform autoregressively at the sample level,

\[ p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{c}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p\big(x_t \mid x_{<t}, \mathbf{c}\big) \tag{31.3}\]

using stacked dilated causal convolutions whose dilation doubles with depth (\(1, 2, 4, \ldots, 512\), repeated), so the receptive field grows exponentially while depth grows linearly. Each layer applies a gated activation \(\mathbf{z} = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_f * \mathbf{x}) \odot \sigma(\mathbf{W}_g * \mathbf{x})\) with residual and skip connections, and conditioning \(\mathbf{c}\) (linguistic features, or mel) is injected through learned upsampling.

The decisive detail is the output distribution: a 256-way categorical softmax over \(\mu\)-law-companded 8-bit samples. A categorical distribution can represent an arbitrary multi-modal \(p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\); a Gaussian cannot. This is strategy 2 of Section 31.2.2 applied at the sample level, and it is why WaveNet escaped the over-smoothing trap that had bounded parametric synthesis for a decade. The quality difference was not incremental — it was the first time synthetic speech stopped sounding synthetic.

The cost is Equation 31.3 itself. At 24 kHz, one second of audio requires 24,000 sequential forward passes. This is not a FLOPs problem that a larger GPU solves; it is a serialisation problem, immune to parallelism. Original implementations ran orders of magnitude slower than real time.

Every subsequent vocoder is an answer to how do I keep WaveNet’s quality without its sequential depth?, and there are only four answers.

The fourth won. It is worth understanding why: mel-to-waveform is nearly deterministic — the conditioning is so strong that little remains to be modelled beyond realistic high-frequency detail and phase. The risk of mode collapse, the usual objection to GANs, is correspondingly small, while the benefit of a single forward pass is large.

31.5.2 HiFi-GAN

HiFi-GAN (Kong et al. 2020) is the load-bearing paper of this era, and its design is a good example of inductive bias chosen from signal structure rather than from architectural fashion. Two ideas:

Multi-Receptive Field Fusion in the generator: parallel residual blocks with different kernel sizes and dilation rates, summed, so each upsampling stage observes several time scales at once.

Multi-Period Discriminator: reshape the 1-D waveform into 2-D by taking every \(p\)-th sample for \(p \in \{2, 3, 5, 7, 11\}\) — co-prime, to avoid overlapping periodicities — and discriminate each reshaped view. Voiced speech is quasi-periodic at the fundamental frequency, so a discriminator that explicitly examines periodic subsequences is directly sensitive to pitch structure, which is exactly what earlier vocoders got wrong. This runs alongside MelGAN’s multi-scale discriminator on progressively average-pooled audio.

The generator objective, in least-squares GAN form, is

\[ \mathcal{L}_G = \sum_{k} \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}(G; D_k) \;+\; \lambda_{\text{fm}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} \;+\; \lambda_{\text{mel}} \big\| \phi(\mathbf{x}) - \phi(G(\mathbf{s})) \big\|_1 \tag{31.4}\]

where \(\phi\) is the mel transform and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}\) is a feature-matching loss: the \(L_1\) distance between the discriminators’ intermediate activations on real and generated audio. The mel term deserves comment given Section 31.2.1 — an \(L_1\) reconstruction loss is exactly what we argued causes over-smoothing. It is safe here only because the adversarial term dominates perceptually and the conditioning is nearly deterministic; the \(L_1\) term acts as a stabiliser and a coarse spectral anchor rather than as the objective.

HiFi-GAN reached WaveNet-level quality at hundreds of times real time on GPU, and faster than real time on CPU. Its practical effect was to end waveform generation as a research bottleneck: after 2020, TTS papers stopped reporting vocoder quality and simply used HiFi-GAN.

Successors. BigVGAN (Lee et al. 2023) pursues universality — any speaker, language, or non-speech audio, unseen in training — by giving the generator a periodic inductive bias through the Snake activation \(f(x) = x + \frac{1}{\alpha}\sin^2(\alpha x)\), and surrounding it with low-pass-filtered up/downsampling to suppress the aliasing that any pointwise nonlinearity introduces. Vocos (Siuzdak 2024) takes the opposite route: predict Fourier coefficients at the STFT frame rate with a ConvNeXt backbone and apply a single inverse STFT, eliminating transposed convolutions and the deep upsampling stack entirely. Both are now more common in new systems than HiFi-GAN itself, and Vocos’s “iSTFT head” is the default fast decoder.

31.6 Autoregressive Acoustic Models

31.6.1 Tacotron and Tacotron 2

Tacotron (Wang et al. 2017) replaced the entire classical pipeline — text analysis, duration model, \(F_0\) model — with a single attentional encoder-decoder mapping characters to spectrogram frames, trained on \(\langle \text{text}, \text{audio}\rangle\) pairs alone. No phoneme durations, no forced alignment, no linguistic feature engineering. In 2017 this was a genuinely surprising result.

Tacotron 2 (Shen et al. 2018) made it production-grade with three changes: predict 80-band log-mel rather than a linear spectrogram and hand it to a modified WaveNet vocoder, establishing the two-stage mel interface that would dominate for six years; use location-sensitive attention, in which the attention energy is computed from cumulative attention weights of previous decoder steps as well as content, giving the model an implicit sense of how far through the text it has progressed; and add a stop-token predictor so the model chooses its own output length. It remains the most-cited acoustic model ever published.

Note that the attention mechanism here is doing the job that CTC and RNN-T do in recognition (Section 28.6): establishing correspondence between two sequences of different lengths. But where an ASR decoder is constrained by the structure of the loss to consume its input monotonically, Tacotron’s attention has no such constraint, and that difference proved expensive.

31.6.2 The Attention Failure Modes

This is the most important negative result in TTS, and it is worth stating carefully because the field re-learned it a second time in Section 31.9.3.

A soft content-based attention over text, driven autoregressively by the model’s own generated audio, is a free-running feedback loop with no monotonicity constraint. Nothing in the architecture or the loss requires the model to consume each input token exactly once, in order. The observed pathologies:

Why these were fatal in production, rather than merely annoying:

  • The degradation is catastrophic, not graceful. You do not get slightly worse prosody; you get a missing word. Dropping “not” from a sentence, or a digit from an address, is a correctness bug.
  • They are invisible to the training loss. Teacher forcing never exposes the model to its own drifting attention. This is exposure bias, and it means the failure cannot be detected by watching the loss curve.
  • They are input-dependent and survive held-out evaluation. Long inputs, repeated words, numerals, acronyms, and unusual punctuation trigger them, so a clean test-set score coexists with regular failures on real user text.
  • The cost is unbounded. A per-utterance failure rate in the low single digits is intolerable when synthesising millions of utterances a day.

Everything in the next section is a response to this table.

31.7 Explicit Duration and Non-Autoregressive TTS

31.7.1 FastSpeech: Robustness by Construction

FastSpeech (Ren et al. 2019) removes attention from the decoder entirely. Given phoneme hidden states \(\mathbf{h}_{1:N}\) and integer durations \(\mathbf{d}_{1:N}\), a length regulator simply repeats each state according to its duration,

\[ \tilde{\mathbf{h}} = \operatorname{LR}(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{d}) = \big[\underbrace{\mathbf{h}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_1}_{d_1}, \underbrace{\mathbf{h}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_2}_{d_2}, \ldots\big], \qquad \big|\tilde{\mathbf{h}}\big| = \sum_{i=1}^{N} d_i \tag{31.5}\]

and a feed-forward Transformer predicts all mel frames in parallel. Durations are obtained from a teacher autoregressive model’s attention matrix, and a duration predictor is trained in the log domain with squared error.

The headline was a roughly \(270\times\) speedup in mel generation, but the substance is elsewhere. Because Equation 31.5 constructs the output by expansion, the alignment is monotonic, surjective, and complete by construction: every input token is consumed, exactly once, in order. Skipping, repeating, and babbling are not reduced in frequency — they are structurally impossible. And the same mechanism yields free controllability: scale all \(d_i\) by \(\alpha\) to change speaking rate, or edit individual \(d_i\) to lengthen a particular phoneme.

Compare Section 28.6.1: CTC also enforces monotonicity structurally, by constraining the set of alignments the loss marginalises over. The same insight, applied in the opposite direction.

31.7.2 FastSpeech 2 and the Variance Adaptor

FastSpeech 2 (Ren et al. 2021) drops the teacher — durations come from the Montreal Forced Aligner and targets are real rather than distilled mels — and contributes the canonical implementation of strategy 1 from Section 31.2.2.

The variance adaptor extracts ground-truth duration, pitch, and energy at training time, embeds them, and feeds them to the decoder as additional conditioning, while training separate predictors for each. At inference the predictors supply the values. The mechanism is exactly Section 31.2.1 in reverse: by conditioning on the variables that explain most of the variation in \(p(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{p})\), the residual conditional becomes closer to unimodal, and a regression loss becomes a less inappropriate model of it.

The pattern — predict the explanatory factors, then condition on them — turned out to be extraordinarily general. Every controllable TTS system has a variance-adaptor-shaped component, and one can read modern prompt-based zero-shot synthesis as a variance adaptor whose conditioning variable is an entire audio recording.

31.7.3 Monotonic Alignment Search

FastSpeech 2 still needs an external aligner. Glow-TTS (Kim et al. 2020) removes that dependency by learning the alignment inside a likelihood model, and the resulting algorithm is the one modern systems use.

A text encoder produces per-token prior statistics \((\mu_i, \sigma_i)\). A flow-based decoder \(f\) maps a mel spectrogram \(\mathbf{M}\) to a latent \(\mathbf{z} = f^{-1}(\mathbf{M})\). For a monotonic surjective alignment \(A\) mapping mel frame \(j\) to text position \(A(j)\), the exact log-likelihood is available because \(f\) is invertible:

\[ \log p(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{p}, A) = \sum_{j} \log \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{z}_j; \, \mu_{A(j)}, \sigma_{A(j)}\big) + \log \left| \det \frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial \mathbf{M}} \right| \tag{31.6}\]

The alignment maximising this is found by dynamic programming over the monotonic lattice — a Viterbi recursion in which each frame either stays on the current token or advances to the next:

\[ Q_{i,j} = \max\big(Q_{i-1,\,j-1}, \; Q_{i,\,j-1}\big) + \log \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{z}_j; \, \mu_i, \sigma_i\big) \tag{31.7}\]

Training alternates: run Monotonic Alignment Search with gradients detached to get \(A^{\star}\), update the likelihood given \(A^{\star}\), and train a duration predictor on \(d_i = |\{j : A^{\star}(j) = i\}|\) for use at inference.

MAS delivers the robustness of explicit duration with the self-sufficiency of end-to-end training, and it is the piece that VITS, Grad-TTS, and Matcha-TTS all adopt unchanged. Readers who found Equation 28.8 familiar will recognise the shape of Equation 31.7: both are dynamic programs over monotonic alignment lattices, differing in whether they marginalise or maximise.

31.7.4 VITS: One Model, One Objective

VITS (Kim et al. 2021) is the most elegant architecture in TTS and repays being read in full. It composes four ideas into a single trainable system.

A conditional VAE whose posterior encoder \(q_\phi(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x}_{\text{lin}})\) reads the linear spectrogram — more information than mel — and produces a frame-level latent \(\mathbf{z}\); a HiFi-GAN-style decoder maps \(\mathbf{z}\) directly to the waveform, so there is no mel bottleneck and no separate vocoder. A normalising-flow prior, which transforms a text-conditioned Gaussian into a more expressive distribution so the KL term does not force \(\mathbf{z}\) to be simple. MAS, run in the flow-transformed space, supplying alignment. And adversarial training, since the reconstruction is now at the waveform level where likelihood is the wrong objective. The bound is

\[ \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{p}) \;\geq\; \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x})}\Big[ \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}) - \log \tfrac{q_\phi(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x})}{p_\theta(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{p}, A)} \Big] \tag{31.8}\]

trained in practice as \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{mel}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{dur}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{fm}}\).

One further component is easy to overlook and conceptually important: the stochastic duration predictor, a flow-based model of \(p(\mathbf{d} \mid \mathbf{p})\). Because duration is sampled rather than predicted, repeated synthesis of the same sentence yields different rhythms — restoring a dimension of natural variability that deterministic duration prediction removes. It is strategy 3 applied to timing.

VITS was the first single-stage parallel system to beat the two-stage Tacotron 2 + HiFi-GAN cascade on naturalness while being faster, and it is the ancestor of most open-source “just works” TTS. Its real pedagogical value is as a demonstration that VAEs, flows, adversarial training, and dynamic-programming alignment are complementary rather than competing tools.

31.8 Diffusion and Flow Matching

31.8.1 From Diffusion to Straight Paths

Diffusion arrived in audio through the vocoder: DiffWave (Kong et al. 2021) and WaveGrad (Chen et al. 2021), independently and near-simultaneously, applied denoising diffusion (Ho et al. 2020) to raw waveform generation. WaveGrad’s conditioning on the continuous noise level rather than a discrete step index was the important detail, since it allows the number of sampling steps to be chosen at inference — the first indication that audio tolerates aggressive step reduction better than images do.

Grad-TTS (Popov et al. 2021) moved diffusion to the acoustic level with an idea that recurs throughout this section: rather than diffusing toward \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), diffuse toward \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{enc}}, \mathbf{I})\), where \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{enc}}\) is the text encoder’s frame-aligned mean (alignment by MAS). Starting the generative process from something already text-aligned means far fewer steps are needed to reach the data.

Flow matching (Lipman et al. 2023) then largely displaced diffusion. Rather than learning to reverse a stochastic corruption, regress a vector field along a straight path between noise \(\mathbf{x}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and data \(\mathbf{x}_1\):

\[ \mathbf{x}_t = \big(1 - (1-\sigma_{\min})t\big)\mathbf{x}_0 + t\,\mathbf{x}_1, \qquad \mathbf{u}_t = \mathbf{x}_1 - (1-\sigma_{\min})\mathbf{x}_0 \]

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1} \big\| v_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{u}_t \big\|^2 \tag{31.9}\]

Training is as simple as diffusion — a regression against a known target — but the probability paths are far straighter, so an ODE solver \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = v_\theta\) needs dozens of steps rather than hundreds, and single digits after distillation. Since Equation 31.9 is a regression loss, it is worth being explicit about why Section 31.2.1 does not apply: the model regresses a vector field, not the data, and the multi-modality of \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{c})\) is carried by the sampled starting point and the trajectory rather than by the per-step target.

Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans 2021) is used almost universally. Train with the conditioning dropped some fraction of the time, then extrapolate at inference:

\[ \tilde{v} = v_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \varnothing) + \alpha \big( v_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c}) - v_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \varnothing) \big) \tag{31.10}\]

It sharpens intelligibility and prompt adherence at the cost of reduced diversity and doubled inference cost — a trade-off worth remembering, since a recurring theme of recent work is buying the benefit back through distillation.

31.8.2 Voicebox: Synthesis as Infilling

Voicebox (Le et al. 2023) reframes the task in a way that turned out to matter more than the specific model. Rather than “generate speech from text”, the training task is text-guided speech infilling: given a masked mel \(\mathbf{m} \odot \mathbf{x}\), the frame-aligned phoneme sequence, and the mask \(\mathbf{m}\), fill in the missing frames, trained with Equation 31.9.

Three consequences follow.

First, infilling unifies tasks that were previously separate. Zero-shot voice cloning is infilling where the unmasked region is a reference recording. Speech editing is infilling where the unmasked region is the surrounding context. Noise removal, continuation, and diverse resampling are all the same model under different masks.

Second, flow matching gives diffusion-level quality at a fraction of the NFE.

Third — and this is the structural point — Voicebox was the first strong demonstration that a non-autoregressive model could match autoregressive codec language models at in-context voice cloning while being roughly an order of magnitude faster and structurally incapable of the failure modes in Table 31.5. Because the output length is fixed in advance by a separate duration model, there is no feedback loop to run away.

Matcha-TTS (Mehta et al. 2024) is the compact statement of the same idea — encoder plus MAS-derived durations for an informative prior, a 1-D U-Net decoder, optimal-transport conditional flow matching — and is the cleanest minimal implementation to read.

31.8.3 Implicit Alignment: E2 TTS and F5-TTS

E2 TTS (Eskimez et al. 2024) then deleted almost everything this chapter has built up. No phonemiser, no aligner, no duration model, no grapheme-to-phoneme step. The input is the raw character sequence, right-padded with filler tokens until its length equals the target mel length; the model is a vanilla flow-matching Transformer performing mel infilling. Alignment is learned implicitly by attention, the only structural hint being that the two sequences have equal length.

That this matches Voicebox is a genuinely surprising negative result, and it appears to reverse the lesson of Section 31.7.1. It does not, and the reason is worth stating precisely: E2 TTS is non-autoregressive. Removing explicit alignment from a model whose output length is fixed in advance does not reintroduce a feedback loop; it merely makes alignment a soft learned quantity. The Era-3 catastrophe required autoregression and unconstrained attention together.

F5-TTS (Y. Chen et al. 2024) fixes E2 TTS’s slow convergence with a Diffusion Transformer backbone and ConvNeXt blocks that refine the text representation before it meets the audio stream, and adds Sway Sampling, a non-uniform schedule for the flow time \(t\) that concentrates function evaluations where the trajectory is most informative — improving quality at fixed NFE with no retraining. Trained on 95k hours of Emilia (He et al. 2024) and released openly with a hackable codebase, it became the default research baseline of 2025.

31.9 Codec Tokens and Speech Language Models

31.9.1 Speech as a Sequence of Integers

Section 27.8.2 observed that clustering a self-supervised encoder yields discrete semantic tokens. Neural audio codecs supply the complementary piece. A codec encodes audio to a low-rate latent, quantises it with a first codebook, quantises the residual with a second, and so on for \(N_q\) levels — residual vector quantisation, introduced for audio by SoundStream (Zeghidour et al. 2022) and popularised by EnCodec (Défossez et al. 2023) and DAC (Kumar et al. 2023). The result is a grid \(\mathbf{C} \in \{1..V\}^{T_c \times N_q}\) of integers: codebook 1 carries most of the content, later codebooks progressively finer acoustic detail.

Once speech is a sequence of integers from a finite vocabulary, the entire language-modelling toolkit applies unchanged — pretrained weights, scaling laws, KV caching, streaming, sampling, and preference optimisation. That is the whole argument for this paradigm, and it is a strong one.

Two costs come with it. The grid is two-dimensional, and a language model emits one token at a time, so the time \(\times\) depth structure must be serialised somehow; the resulting design space (flattening, delay patterns, depth transformers, parallel heads) is the subject of Section 30.8. And quantisation imposes a fidelity ceiling that no amount of modelling recovers.

31.9.2 AudioLM and the Semantic/Acoustic Split

AudioLM (Borsos et al. 2023) identified the failure mode of naive codec language modelling and the fix. A flat LM over acoustic tokens must spend its capacity predicting reverb, microphone noise, and fine spectral texture, and consequently never learns long-range linguistic structure — it produces locally plausible, globally incoherent audio.

The solution is a hierarchy along the information axis rather than the time axis: first model semantic tokens (from a self-supervised encoder, capturing phonetic content while discarding speaker and channel), then generate acoustic tokens conditioned on them, coarse levels before fine. Predicting meaning first makes next-token prediction a linguistic act rather than an acoustic one.

SPEAR-TTS (Kharitonov et al. 2023) showed the split buys something beyond coherence. Splitting synthesis into “reading” (text \(\to\) semantic) and “speaking” (semantic \(\to\) acoustic) decouples the data requirements: speaking needs no transcripts at all, and reading needs very little paired data because its target is a compact semantic sequence. They obtained high-quality synthesis from fifteen minutes of transcribed speech. For low-resource languages this is the central mechanism.

Later codecs collapse the two-tokeniser design into one by distilling a self-supervised model into the first RVQ level, so codebook 1 is semantic and the rest acoustic — SpeechTokenizer (Zhang et al. 2024) and Moshi’s Mimi (Défossez et al. 2024) both do this.

31.9.3 VALL-E and In-Context Voice Cloning

VALL-E (Wang et al. 2023) cast TTS as conditional codec language modelling and trained on 60k hours — roughly a thousand times more than contemporary TTS corpora. Over EnCodec’s eight-codebook grid it uses two models: an autoregressive Transformer for codebook 1, and a non-autoregressive Transformer for codebooks 2–8, each conditioned on all shallower levels:

\[ p(\mathbf{C} \mid \mathbf{y}, \tilde{\mathbf{C}}) = \underbrace{\prod_{t} p\big(c_{t,1} \mid c_{<t,1}, \tilde{\mathbf{C}}, \mathbf{y}\big)}_{\text{autoregressive over time}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\prod_{j=2}^{N_q} p\big(c_{:,j} \mid c_{:,<j}, \tilde{\mathbf{C}}, \mathbf{y}\big)}_{\text{parallel over depth}} \tag{31.11}\]

Zero-shot voice cloning then requires no architectural mechanism at all: prepend the tokens of a three-second enrolment clip \(\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\) and its transcript, and let the model continue. Because it is literally continuing that recording, it preserves not only timbre but emotion, speaking style, and acoustic environment — things a speaker embedding cannot carry.

And it reintroduced every failure mode in Table 31.5. An autoregressive model over a token grid has no monotonicity constraint with respect to text, so word repetition, skipping, and — a new variant — infinite silence loops under low-temperature sampling all returned. The silence loop is instructive: greedy or low-\(p\) decoding finds that silence is the highest-probability continuation, and the model never escapes.

The field’s response reproduces, in compressed form, the history of Section 31.7. VALL-E 2 (S. Chen et al. 2024) adds repetition-aware sampling, switching adaptively between nucleus and random sampling when repetition is detected, plus grouped code modelling to shorten the sequence. Others reintroduce explicit monotonic constraints at inference or predict phoneme position alongside audio. This is the same lesson being learned twice, and pointing that out is the single most useful thing a textbook can do with this material.

31.9.4 The CosyVoice Template

The dominant industrial architecture of 2024–2026 is a hybrid that takes the best of both halves of this chapter: an autoregressive LM over semantic tokens, followed by non-autoregressive flow matching to mel, followed by a vocoder (Du, Chen, et al. 2024; Du, Wang, et al. 2024).

The reasoning is that the two stages have different requirements. Long-range linguistic and prosodic structure benefits from autoregression and from inheriting a pretrained text LLM; acoustic detail benefits from parallel generation and does not need to be sampled sequentially. Placing the AR/NAR boundary at the semantic/acoustic boundary lets each do what it is good at.

Two details from CosyVoice 2 are worth carrying forward. Its speech tokeniser is derived from an ASR encoder, so tokens are supervised to be semantic rather than emerging from unsupervised clustering — you get to choose what “semantic” means. And it replaces RVQ with finite scalar quantisation, which bounds and rounds each latent dimension independently so that every code is reachable by construction, eliminating the codebook collapse that Equation 27.4 was invented to prevent. CosyVoice 3 (Du et al. 2025) scales the recipe to a million hours.

31.9.5 The Continuous Counter-Current

It is worth resisting the conclusion that discretisation is necessary. MELLE (Meng et al. 2024) performs autoregressive synthesis of continuous mel frames, with no vector quantisation anywhere. Cross-entropy being unavailable, it combines a regression loss with a variational sampling module — so the model samples rather than predicting a conditional mean, addressing Equation 31.2 directly — and a spectrogram flux loss that penalises small frame-to-frame variation, explicitly punishing the degenerate static output a regression loss invites.

The result is single-stage, free of the quantisation ceiling, and more robust than VALL-E. That last point is the reason to teach it: it isolates which of VALL-E’s problems come from autoregression (few) and which from discrete-token sampling (most). By 2026 this “continuous autoregression with a small generative head” family is a credible alternative rather than a curiosity, and the question of discrete versus continuous representations remains genuinely open — as it does on the representation-learning side (Section 27.9).

31.9.6 Streaming and Duplex Speech

Conversational systems are latency-bound in a way that offline synthesis is not, and the relevant metric is not the real-time factor but the time to first audio.

Moshi (Défossez et al. 2024) contributed several mechanisms now standard. Its Mimi codec runs at 12.5 Hz with a semantic first codebook. Its RQ-Transformer pairs a large temporal backbone with a small depth transformer that autoregresses over codebook levels within a frame — the standard answer to serialising the token grid. Its inner monologue predicts time-aligned text tokens as a per-frame prefix to the audio tokens, extending AudioLM’s hierarchy up one more level and substantially improving linguistic quality; reversing the sign of the text-audio delay converts the same model between streaming ASR and streaming TTS. And multi-stream modelling of both participants’ audio makes overlap, interruption, and backchannels representable without a turn-taking state machine.

CosyVoice 2 achieves streaming differently, through chunk-aware causal flow matching — attention masks that let a single set of weights serve streaming and offline synthesis by training over a mixture of chunk sizes.

The 2026 position is that this dichotomy is mostly historical. Frontier systems hybridise: an AR backbone with a small per-token diffusion head, block-wise AR with parallel denoising inside each block, or the AR-semantic/NAR-acoustic split of Section 31.9.4.

31.10 Zero-Shot Voice Cloning

Cloning a voice from seconds of audio, with no fine-tuning, went from research goal to commodity in about three years. The mechanism changed four times, and the progression is a good summary of the chapter.

The trajectory is from conditioning on a vector to conditioning on an example to conditioning on a description, which mirrors the corresponding shift in text and image generation almost exactly.

The safety consequences are not incidental to the technology, and a chapter that described the capability without them would be incomplete. Three-second cloning from open-weight models is now routine. Countermeasures exist — audio watermarking with localised detection, and provenance manifests — but their limits are real and acknowledged by their authors: watermarking is voluntary, there is no interoperable standard, marks are removable and forgeable, and robustness degrades as an attacker gains knowledge of the algorithm or access to the detector. Passive detection of unwatermarked synthetic speech remains substantially unsolved.

31.11 The Open-Source Landscape

Zero-shot synthesis with a flow-matching model is a few lines:

from f5_tts.api import F5TTS

tts = F5TTS()                     # DiT backbone + Vocos vocoder
wav, sr, _ = tts.infer(
    ref_file="reference.wav",     # ~5–10 s of the target voice
    ref_text="Text spoken in the reference clip.",
    gen_text="Text to synthesise in that voice.",
    nfe_step=32,                  # ODE steps: quality/latency knob
    cfg_strength=2.0,             # classifier-free guidance weight
)

The two arguments worth experimenting with are nfe_step and cfg_strength, since they expose Equation 31.9 and Equation 31.10 directly: fewer steps trade quality for latency, and stronger guidance trades diversity for intelligibility.

31.12 Evaluation

In recognition, evaluation is contested at the margins — text normalisation, test-set selection — but the metric itself is sound, because a unique correct answer exists. In synthesis there is no correct answer, so evaluation is a research problem in its own right, and one that the field currently considers to be in a poor state.

31.12.1 Subjective Evaluation and the Limits of MOS

MOS (Mean Opinion Score) presents stimuli one at a time for rating on a 1–5 scale and averages. CMOS presents pairs for a preference judgement on a \(-3 \ldots +3\) scale. SMOS rates similarity to a reference speaker.

MOS has been the field’s primary metric for thirty years, and the case against it is now well documented and broadly accepted. The central finding (Maguer et al. 2024) is that MOS, despite presenting itself as an absolute category rating, behaves as a relative measure. Re-running listening tests on identical stimuli with different accompanying systems shifts scores by up to a full point, and moves system pairs across the threshold of statistical significance. Listeners construct their anchors from whatever they have already heard, so the composition of the test set determines the numbers.

The practical consequence is stark and worth stating without hedging:

WarningMOS values from different papers cannot be compared

This is the explicit conclusion of the community guidelines (Cooper et al. 2025), and it invalidates a large fraction of the comparison tables in the TTS literature. If you want to compare against a system, you must synthesise with it and include it in your own listening test.

Those guidelines, an output of a Dagstuhl seminar and written partly as instructions for reviewers, are the closest thing to an official position. Their other recommendations:

  • Naturalness MOS is saturated. Modern systems cluster at the ceiling, so the paradigm cannot resolve them. Prefer more discriminative designs: AB/ABX pairwise, best-worst scaling, or MUSHRA-like tests — while noting that a true MUSHRA test is impossible for TTS, since there are no anchors.
  • Report the protocol: number of listeners, samples per system, ratings per sample, the exact question asked, the scale, and the statistical test.
  • Use appropriate statistics. MOS data are ordinal, so non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon, Mann-Whitney) are appropriate.
  • Control the confounds: loudness-normalise stimuli, and be aware that listeners’ preference for a particular voice confounds naturalness ratings when systems use different speakers.
  • Never call a predicted score “MOS.” Call it predicted MOS.

31.12.2 Objective Metrics

Two entries deserve elaboration.

WER as an intelligibility proxy has a revealing pathology: human recordings frequently score worse than good synthetic speech. That should immediately tell you it is measuring how easy the audio is for an ASR model to transcribe, not how intelligible it is to a person. It remains useful — it catches the catastrophic failures of Table 31.5 reliably — but it should be read as a robustness check rather than a quality measure. Reporting under two different ASR families guards against a system being flattered by sharing a lineage with its evaluator.

FAD is manipulable. Its finite-sample covariance estimate biases the score by \(O(1/N)\), which means you can improve your reported FAD simply by generating more samples. That is an improvement obtainable with no change to the model.

WarningOptimising these metrics directly backfires

Using WER as a reinforcement-learning reward measurably improves the metric while collapsing prosody toward monotone delivery. It is a clean instance of reward hacking outside the text modality: the reward captures intelligibility and is silent about everything else that makes speech worth listening to.

31.12.3 Datasets and Protocols

The seed-tts-eval protocol — reference clip, prompt transcript, target text; report WER and speaker similarity — is what most 2025–2026 papers quote. Its hard split is the most honest number in the field: frontier character error rates there run around 6–8%, roughly an order of magnitude worse than on the easy splits.

A newer generation of benchmarks targets what single-sentence naturalness cannot measure: complex prosody and expressiveness (Manku et al. 2025) and natural-language instruction following (Huang et al. 2025), both typically judged by a large audio-language model rather than by human raters — a practice that is spreading quickly and has itself received almost no meta-evaluation.

31.12.4 The Comparability Problem

A 2025 position paper (C. Wang et al. 2025) documents that the standard reporting set — WER, similarity, MOS, and real-time factor — is failing simultaneously, and its most concrete finding deserves quoting directly. Papers reporting “WER on LibriSpeech test-clean” have used 1,234 samples, 40 samples, and 1,127 samples with punctuation preserved, among other choices. These are not the same measurement. A 40-sample evaluation has enormous variance and is not comparable to a 1,234-sample one, yet both appear in tables as a single number under a single column heading.

Similar problems affect the rest of the set. Similarity protocols differ on whether the prompt segment is included and on which speaker encoder is used, and saturate above a threshold beyond which further gains are imperceptible. Real-time factors are reported without batch size, hardware, vocoder, or streaming status. And test-set contamination is a live concern, since LibriSpeech is both the primary TTS benchmark and plausibly present in the pre-training corpora of the text LLMs that modern systems are built on (Tseng et al. 2025).

The defensible practice in 2026 is therefore: run your own listening test with all baselines included; pin a public protocol for objective numbers; add a distributional metric with validated human correlation; and treat any cross-paper comparison you did not run yourself as unreliable.

31.13 Open Problems

  1. Long-form consistency. Single-sentence quality is essentially solved; multi-minute coherence is not. Speaker drift, prosodic discontinuity at sentence boundaries, and the loss of discourse-level structure — given/new information, contrastive stress, paragraph pacing — all persist. There is no accepted metric for prosodic coherence over minutes, which is part of why there is little progress.
  2. Controllable expressivity. Producing expressive speech is largely solved by prompting and scale. Specifying it is not. Compositional control (happy and whispered and emphasising this word) and context-inferred delivery, where correct prosody must be deduced from surrounding text rather than stated, both remain weak.
  3. Robustness in autoregressive models. The Table 31.5 catalogue is reduced but not eliminated, and no method offers a guarantee — only a lower rate. The hard splits of current benchmarks are where this shows.
  4. Evaluation. Arguably the rate-limiting problem for the whole field. Open: a subjective protocol with resolution above the human-parity ceiling; objective metrics validated on modern systems; contamination-free refreshed benchmarks; and meta-evaluation of the audio-LLM judges now being adopted quickly and uncritically.
  5. Latency versus quality in duplex systems. First-packet latencies below 100 ms are achievable, but full-duplex conversation exposes a frontier where low latency buys spurious interruptions and conservative endpointing buys sluggishness. No system is simultaneously fast, non-interrupting, and reliably yielding.
  6. Code-switching and the long tail. Massively multilingual synthesis has advanced quickly, but intra-sentential code-switching with native-like pronunciation in both languages, accent leakage from the reference voice, and grapheme-to-phoneme conversion for languages without a phonemiser all remain open — as does evaluating any of it, since recruiting native listeners for long-tail languages is often impossible.
  7. Safety and provenance. Standardised, interoperable, adversarially robust watermarking; attribution of which model produced a sample rather than merely that it is synthetic; and passive detection of unwatermarked output.
  8. What makes a representation learnable? Discrete tokens, VAE latents, mel, and waveform latents are all in current use, and there is evidence that better reconstruction fidelity in a learned representation does not predict better synthesis quality from it. Nobody has a principled account of what makes a speech representation good to generate into, as opposed to merely invertible. This strikes me as the most interesting open scientific question in the area.
  9. Scaling laws are unmapped. There is no compute-optimal frontier for speech generation analogous to those established for text, no clear picture of how quality trades off between hours, parameters, and token rate. This is not independent of problem 4: you cannot fit a scaling law to a quantity you cannot measure reliably.

31.14 Summary

Speech synthesis is organised by one fact: the mapping from text to speech is one-to-many, so there is no correct output to regress toward. Minimising squared error returns the conditional mean of a multi-modal distribution, which corresponds to no real utterance and sounds muffled and lifeless. This diagnosis was made in 2009 for parametric synthesis and has driven every subsequent architectural decision.

Three strategies answer it, and the field’s history is their interleaving. WaveNet applied a categorical output distribution at the sample level and demonstrated that generative modelling, not better regression, was the missing ingredient — at a sequential cost that GAN vocoders later removed entirely. Tacotron showed a single attentional model could learn text-to-acoustics alignment unsupervised, and then showed why unconstrained attention driven by autoregression is catastrophic in production. FastSpeech made monotonic alignment structural rather than learned, and FastSpeech 2 made the one-to-many problem explicit by conditioning on the variables that explain the variance. Glow-TTS learned that alignment inside a likelihood model with monotonic alignment search, and VITS composed VAE, flow, adversarial training, and MAS into a single objective. Flow matching then made non-autoregressive generation cheap enough to dominate, and Voicebox’s reframing of synthesis as infilling unified voice cloning, editing, and continuation into one training task. Codec tokens made speech a language-modelling problem, which bought in-context voice cloning and the whole LLM toolchain — and which promptly reintroduced the autoregressive failure modes the field had spent 2019 to 2021 eliminating.

Two threads are worth carrying into the following chapters. The token grid that Section 31.9 treats as given is the subject of Chapter 30, where residual quantisation, codebook collapse, frame rate, and the serialisation of a two-dimensional grid into a one-dimensional sequence get the treatment they deserve. And the streaming, duplex, text-and-audio-interleaved models sketched in Section 31.9.6 are the architecture of the speech language models of Chapter 32, which close this part of the book.

The honest summary of the current state is that synthesis quality on short, clean, single-sentence inputs is at or near the ceiling of what listening tests can resolve — and that this is as much a statement about the tests as about the models.

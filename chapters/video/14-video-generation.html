15  Video Generation

Video generation is a generalisation of image generation: a model samples a clip \(\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times H \times W \times 3}\) from a distribution \(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{V} \mid \mathbf{c})\) conditioned on some control signal \(\mathbf{c}\) — most commonly a text prompt, but also an image (image-to-video), a previous clip (extension), a reference identity (subject-driven generation), or a structured pose / depth / camera trajectory (control-net style generation).

An image is the degenerate case \(T = 1\), so anything that works for image generation is a candidate for video. But the temporal axis adds two new requirements that change the engineering substantially:

  • Temporal consistency. Independently generated frames flicker: identities drift, lighting jumps, objects re-appear with different textures. Modelling joint dependencies across frames is what separates a real video model from a slideshow of images.
  • Sequence length. A 5-second 720p clip at 24 FPS has \(120 \times 720 \times 1280 \times 3 \approx 3.3 \times 10^8\) pixels. Even with a 8 × 8 × 4 latent VAE, a denoiser operates on \(\sim 10^6\) tokens per clip. Memory and compute become the dominant engineering constraints; almost every video-generation paper of 2022–2026 is a memory trick in some form.

The 2022–2026 landscape splits naturally along two axes:

  1. What is the denoiser? U-Net + temporal layers (the inherited image-diffusion stack, 2022–2024) vs Diffusion Transformer (DiT) (Peebles & Xie (Peebles and Xie 2023); the dominant 2024–2026 design, used by Sora, Stable Video Diffusion 3, CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo).
  2. How is video conditioning added? Three families, corresponding to how much training the video data sees:
    • Train from scratch on video data (VDM, Imagen Video, Sora, CogVideoX).
    • Fine-tune a pretrained image model by adding temporal layers, sometimes only training those (Make-a-Video, Video LDM / Align-Your-Latents, Stable Video Diffusion).
    • Train-free adaptation of a pretrained image model — no weight updates, only inference-time tricks (Text2Video-Zero).

This chapter follows that taxonomy. We first review the diffusion fundamentals (Section 15.1), then walk through the three families in order of increasing training cost (Section 15.2), discuss the orthogonal autoregressive line (Section 15.3), survey the closed-source flagships (Section 15.4) and the open-weights landscape (Section 15.5), and close with the evaluation question (Section 15.6).

Note

The focus of these notes is video understanding. This chapter is included so that a video-VLM engineer reading the rest of the book has the vocabulary to follow a generation paper, recognise the shared components (VAE, T5/CLIP encoder, DiT backbone), and pick a sensible evaluation setup. It is not a substitute for a full generative-models text.

15.1 Diffusion Fundamentals

Almost every video generator in this chapter is a diffusion model. This section recaps the minimum prerequisite vocabulary. Readers familiar with image diffusion can skim straight to Section 15.2.

15.1.1 Denoising Diffusion

Diffusion models (Ho et al. 2020; Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015) define a forward noising process that gradually turns a real sample \(\mathbf{x}_0\) into Gaussian noise \(\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})\) over \(T\) steps:

\[ q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \beta_t\mathbf{I}) \tag{15.1}\]

with a variance schedule \(\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\). The forward process has a closed-form marginal, \(\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) with \(\bar{\alpha}_t \;=\; \prod_{s \leq t} (1 - \beta_s)\). A denoiser \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c})\) is trained to predict the noise added at step \(t\), optimising

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \bigl[ \| \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c}) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \|_2^2 \bigr]. \tag{15.2}\]

Sampling reverses the chain: starting from \(\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})\) and iterating \(\mathbf{x}_t \to \mathbf{x}_{t-1}\) using the predicted noise. A 1000- step DDPM trajectory is wasteful; DDIM (Song et al. 2021) replaces it with a deterministic ODE solve and 20–50 steps are typical in 2024– 2026. Faster solvers (DPM-Solver-2 / 3, UniPC) push this to single digits.

Classifier-Free Guidance

Almost all video generators use classifier-free guidance (CFG) (Ho and Salimans 2021): train the denoiser jointly with conditional and unconditional inputs (drop the conditioning \(\mathbf{c}\) with probability ~10 %), then at sampling time blend them:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{c}) \;=\; \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing) \;+\; w \bigl[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{c}) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing)\bigr] \tag{15.3}\]

with guidance scale \(w > 1\) (typically 4–7.5 for image, 6–9 for video). CFG is the lever that trades sample diversity for prompt fidelity.

Latent Diffusion

Running diffusion on raw pixels is prohibitively expensive for video. Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) (Rombach et al. 2022) compress \(\mathbf{x}\) to a low-dimensional latent \(\mathbf{z} = \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})\) via a frozen VAE, run diffusion in \(\mathbf{z}\)-space, and decode back at the end via \(\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_0)\). For video this matters even more: a 3D VAE (space + time, typically 8 × 8 × 4 compression — CogVideoX 1.5 uses 16 × 16 × 4, Sora uses an “undisclosed but aggressive” 3D VAE) reduces a 16 × 720 × 1280 × 3 clip to a 4 × 90 × 160 × 16 latent — a 24× memory saving.

Text Conditioning

Text prompts enter the denoiser via cross-attention from a frozen language encoder. The three dominant choices in 2024–2026:

  • CLIP-text (OpenAI / OpenCLIP) — fast, strong on short prompts, weak on compositional language. The default for the Stable Diffusion 1.x / 2.x line.
  • T5-XXL / Flan-T5-XXL (Raffel et al. 2020) — the heavyweight in Imagen, Imagen Video, DeepFloyd-IF, Sora, and CogVideoX. Excellent at long prompts and compositions, expensive to run.
  • LLM-as-text-encoder — direct hidden states from a Llama-3-class LLM, often distilled into a smaller adapter. Used by HunyuanVideo and several 2025 open-weights models; trades T5’s robustness for better instruction-following.

Denoiser architectures: U-Net vs DiT

For image diffusion, the original choice was a U-Net with cross- attention to text (Ronneberger et al. 2015; Rombach et al. 2022). Peebles & Xie (Peebles and Xie 2023) showed that a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) — a plain transformer over a tokenised latent grid — scales better and beats the U-Net once data and compute are abundant. The video field has followed the same trajectory:

  • 2022–2023 video models (VDM, Make-a-Video, Video LDM, Stable Video Diffusion) used U-Net + factorised space-time attention or 3D convolutions.
  • 2024 onwards (Sora, CogVideoX, Mochi-1, HunyuanVideo, Wan-2.1, LTX-Video, Open-Sora-Plan) uses DiT-style transformers over a 3D-VAE latent.

The DiT wins for the same reason it does in image generation: a plain transformer scales cleanly, parameter-shares across all spatial and temporal positions, and benefits from every infrastructure improvement made for LLMs (FlashAttention, pipeline parallelism, sequence parallelism, etc.).

15.2 Three Families of Video Generation

The video-diffusion approaches of 2022–2026 fall into three families distinguished by how much video training they do.

15.2.1 Family 1 — Train from Scratch on Video

The cleanest baseline: train a denoiser on video data only, with no image pretraining. Used by the biggest closed flagships (Sora) and by recent open-weights efforts that have the compute (CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo). The early open-academic example is VDM.

Video Diffusion Models (VDM, 2022)

VDM (Ho, Salimans, et al. 2022) (Ho et al., 2022) introduced the modern video-diffusion template. The denoiser is a 3D U-Net factorised over space and time:

  • Each spatial 2D convolution is extended to a “space-only 3D convolution” with kernel size \((1, 3, 3)\) — only the spatial axes see the kernel; frames are processed independently.
  • Each spatial self-attention layer is left as is, but with the frames axis treated as a batch dimension.
  • After every spatial self-attention block, a temporal attention block is inserted, with the spatial axes folded into the batch dimension. The temporal positional embedding is a relative one (no absolute notion of time, so the model works across arbitrary clip lengths).

This factorised design — spatial blocks operating on frames in parallel, temporal blocks acting only across time — became the canonical pattern. Almost every video diffusion model of 2022–2024 inherits it; the DiT variants of 2024–2026 generalise it with a single transformer that attends across (t, h, w) jointly but still often uses block-sparse masks that recover the factorised form for compute efficiency.

Imagen Video (2022)

Imagen Video (Ho, Chan, et al. 2022) (Google, 2022) tackled the resolution problem: generating long, high-resolution videos directly is too expensive, so use a cascade of seven diffusion models that progressively refine spatial and temporal resolution:

  1. A base diffusion model produces a low-resolution, short clip (40 × 24 × 24, 16 frames at ~3 FPS).
  2. Three spatial super-resolution (SSR) models progressively upscale: 24×24 → 80×80 → 240×240 → 720×720.
  3. Three temporal super-resolution (TSR) models interpolate in time: 16 frames → 32 → 64 → 128 frames (≈5 s at 24 FPS).

Each stage is conditioned on T5-XXL text embeddings and on the output of the previous stage. The base model is a 3D-conv U-Net with spatio-temporal self-attention; the upsamplers use cheaper alternatives — temporal convolutions instead of attention (linear in frames, not quadratic) and no spatial attention at the highest resolution (where it would dominate compute). The result was the first text-to-video model widely regarded as “production quality” by the standards of 2022, and it set the cascade template that Sora later inherited.

Sora (2024)

Sora (OpenAI 2024) (OpenAI, Feb 2024) is the headline closed-flagship that made transformer-based video generation mainstream. Two architectural breaks with the 2022 line:

  • DiT denoiser. Replace the 3D U-Net with a plain transformer.
  • 3D space-time patches. Instead of 2D patches from a single image, Sora extracts \(p_t \times p_h \times p_w\) spacetime patches (e.g. \(2 \times 16 \times 16\) pixels) from the 3D-VAE-compressed latent. Each patch becomes a token; the transformer applies self- attention across all spacetime tokens to model spatial and temporal dependencies jointly (rather than via the factorised pattern of VDM/Imagen Video).

The conditioning stack is standard — cross-attention from a T5-XXL text encoder, with classifier-free guidance for sampling. The training data and exact 3D-VAE design are not disclosed, but the inferred recipe is: a high-compression-ratio 3D VAE (likely \(8 \times 8 \times 4\) or finer), a billion-parameter DiT, and multi-aspect-ratio / multi-duration training so the same model serves 5-second portrait clips and 60-second landscape clips. Sora 2 (2024) and the disclosed Wan, HunyuanVideo, Mochi, CogVideoX designs follow the same pattern with smaller, public weights.

CogVideoX / CogVideoX 1.5 (Tsinghua + Zhipu AI, 2024)

CogVideoX (Yang et al. 2024) (Aug 2024, 1.5 released Nov 2024) is the canonical open-weights train-from-scratch video DiT. Three contributions:

  • 3D-VAE with \(4 \times 8 \times 8\) compression (1.5 raised this to \(4 \times 16 \times 16\)).
  • Expert Adaptive Layer Normalisation. Conditioning signals (timestep, text) modulate the transformer through dedicated AdaLN “experts” rather than via cross-attention. This was influential — many 2024–2026 video DiTs followed.
  • Multi-resolution / multi-duration training with bucketing, similar to Sora’s inferred recipe but disclosed.

CogVideoX-5B (5 B-parameter DiT) and 1.5-5B are the most-used open references for academic comparisons in 2025–2026.

15.2.2 Family 2 — Fine-tune a Pretrained Image Model

The pragmatic majority of 2022–2024 work. Start from a strong image diffusion model (Stable Diffusion 1.5 / 2.1, eDiff-I, IF) and add temporal layers. Often only the new temporal layers are trained, with the image weights kept frozen — this is cheap, the image model has already learned visual priors, and the resulting generator inherits the image model’s compositional strength.

Make-A-Video (2022)

Make-A-Video (Singer et al. 2022) (Meta, Sep 2022) — the first widely cited example. Composition of three modules:

  1. A pretrained text-to-image diffusion model (eDiff-I-class).
  2. A spatiotemporal super-resolution model that upgrades the image generator to a video generator by adding (2+1)D layers:
    • (2+1)D convolutions: each pretrained 2D conv is followed by a new 1D temporal conv, initialised to the identity. The 2D conv first generates a stack of frames in parallel; the 1D conv smooths across time.
    • (2+1)D attention: each spatial attention layer is followed by a new temporal attention layer that attends only across frames at each spatial position.
  3. A frame interpolation network that increases frame rate.

Only the new temporal modules are trained; the image weights stay frozen, so the model inherits Make-a-Scene-class image quality at roughly the training cost of a temporal-only model. The (2+1)D factorisation became standard for years.

Video LDM / Align-Your-Latents (2023)

Align-Your-Latents (Blattmann, Rombach, et al. 2023) (Blattmann et al., 2023, NVIDIA / LMU Munich) — the canonical latent variant of the Make-A-Video idea. Build on a Stable-Diffusion-style LDM:

  • Insert temporal layers (3D convolutions + temporal attention) between the frozen spatial layers of the image LDM.
  • Train only the temporal layers with the spatial LDM weights frozen.
  • Use a personalised decoder: the original LDM decoder produces flickering artefacts when frames are decoded independently, so a video-aware decoder is fine-tuned — temporal layers are added, spatial encoder weights are kept frozen, and the decoder is trained jointly with a reconstruction loss and a GAN-style 3D-conv patch discriminator that distinguishes real from generated video clips.

Video LDM is the template for Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) (Blattmann, Dockhorn, et al. 2023) (Stability AI, Nov 2023), the most-downloaded open-weights video model of 2023–2024. SVD trains the SD-2.1 U-Net with added temporal layers on a curated 5-million-clip subset of the LVD corpus and produces 14- or 25-frame 1024 × 576 clips. It is the default image-to-video baseline in most 2024 papers.

15.2.3 Family 3 — Train-Free Adaptation

Inference-time tricks that produce video from a pretrained image diffusion model with no weight updates. Cheap, fast, sufficient for short demos, but lacking the realism of trained models. Useful mainly as a baseline and for one-off effects.

Text2Video-Zero (2023)

Text2Video-Zero (Khachatryan et al. 2023) (Picsart AI Research, Mar 2023) — the reference train-free method. Two tricks:

  1. Camera-motion noise initialisation. Start with one random latent \(\mathbf{z}_T\). Run a single DDIM backward step to denoise it slightly. Apply a sequence of translations to the resulting latent (simulating a panning camera). Then take a DDPM forward step to obtain a sequence of noised latents, each related to the first by a known linear shift in latent space. This injects coherent inter-frame motion at the noise level.
  2. Cross-frame attention. Reprogram the U-Net’s self-attention so that each frame’s queries cross-attend (using the image model’s pretrained Q, K, V matrices) to the first frame’s keys and values. The first frame thus acts as an anchor for object identity and background.

The result is short, somewhat coherent video from a frozen image diffusion model. Not competitive with trained models on benchmarks, but a useful diagnostic — when an image diffusion model produces better short clips with these tricks than a poorly-trained video diffusion model produces with full training, the latter has something wrong.

15.3 Autoregressive Video Generation

Orthogonal to the diffusion line is the autoregressive (AR) approach: tokenise video into a sequence of discrete codes and predict the next code. Two variants:

  • Discrete VAE / VQ-VAE tokenisation (Oord et al. 2017; Esser et al. 2021; P. Sun et al. 2024). A VQ-VAE maps each clip to a sequence of integer codes from a fixed codebook (e.g. 16 384 entries). A transformer is trained with a next-token-prediction loss on these codes; sampling decodes from codes back to pixels. The original VQ-VAE-based video generators (Video VQ-VAE, NUWA-XL) date to 2021–2022; the more capable modern examples are below.
  • Continuous AR with a diffusion head (Li et al. 2024; Tian et al. 2024). Predict the parameters of the next frame’s distribution autoregressively, but parameterise that distribution as a small diffusion model. Hybrid; recent and less proven for video specifically.

The autoregressive line is appealing for two reasons:

  • Token interface unification with LLMs. A video tokeniser turns video into the same token stream as text, so a single transformer can read a video and write a video — exactly the understanding + generation unification that Janus-Pro pursued in
  • Inherits LLM infrastructure. Speculative decoding, prefix caching, FlashAttention, pipeline parallelism — every win the LLM community gets transfers for free.

The headline open-source examples:

  • VideoPoet (Kondratyuk et al. 2024) (Google, Dec 2023). The first large-scale (~8B-param) autoregressive video LM. Predicts a unified stream of image tokens + audio tokens + text tokens, enabling text-to-video, image-to-video, video stylisation, and audio-to-video in a single model. Uses MAGVIT-v2 (Yu et al. 2023) as the video tokeniser, which crucially improved tokeniser quality to the point AR video matched diffusion on FVD.
  • LlamaGen (P. Sun et al. 2024) (ByteDance, Jun 2024). Image-only but architecturally the cleanest demonstration: a vanilla LLaMA transformer with a high-quality VQ tokeniser produces images at Stable-Diffusion quality. The video extension is implicit in the Janus-Pro line — Janus-Pro (Chen et al. 2025) uses the same VQ tokeniser approach for image generation, embedded in a unified understanding/generation transformer (see 1).
  • MAGI-1, Cosmos-1 (NVIDIA, late 2024) — recent autoregressive video models for world simulation; smaller community footprint than the diffusion line but increasingly relevant for robotics and embodied AI.

As of mid-2026 the diffusion-DiT line is still ahead on raw video quality (Sora, Veo 3, CogVideoX 1.5), but the AR + diffusion-head hybrids are closing the gap, and the unification argument keeps this line architecturally alive.

15.4 Closed-Source Flagships

Brief mentions to anchor the architecture discussion in the public state of the art. None of these have full disclosed weights but most have technical reports.

  • Sora / Sora 2 (OpenAI 2024) (OpenAI). The reference text-to-video DiT, 1080p, up to ~1 minute. Sora 2 (Sep 2024) adds synchronised audio generation and improved long-clip coherence.
  • Veo / Veo 2 / Veo 3 (Google DeepMind 2024) (Google DeepMind). 4K-capable, 8-second prompts with strong physics. Veo 3 (May 2025) added native audio (dialogue, sound effects) at video-generation time and now matches or exceeds Sora 2 on most evaluation axes.
  • Movie Gen (Polyak et al. 2024) (Meta, Oct 2024). 30B-parameter text-to-video DiT trained on a curated 100M-video corpus; also generates synced audio and allows video editing (text-guided edits to a given clip).
  • Genie / Genie 2 / Genie 3 (Bruce et al. 2024) (Google DeepMind). Action-conditioned world model — given an image, the model generates playable video where the user can control the agent. Conceptually distinct from the text-to-video flagships above — more an interactive world simulator than a film generator.
  • Runway Gen-3 / Gen-4, Pika 2.0, Luma Dream Machine (2024–2025). Commercial generators with strong consumer features (motion brushes, style transfer, character LoRAs) and undisclosed architectural details.

15.5 Open-Source Landscape (2024–2026)

The open-weights line has been moving extremely fast since CogVideoX. The current shortlist, all DiT-based unless noted:

  • CogVideoX-2B / 5B / CogVideoX 1.5-5B (Yang et al. 2024) (Aug 2024 → Nov 2024) — see Section 15.2.1. 5 B-param DiT, Apache-2.0; the academic baseline.
  • Mochi 1 (Genmo AI 2024) (Genmo, Oct 2024) — 10 B-param DiT with asymmetric attention (text tokens get cheaper attention); Apache-2.0. Strong on motion realism.
  • HunyuanVideo (Kong et al. 2024) (Tencent, Dec 2024) — 13 B-param DiT with a dual-stream attention layout (image tokens and text tokens are processed in parallel then fused); Apache-2.0. The first open model to challenge closed-source quality in side-by-side preference tests.
  • Wan 2.1 / Wan 2.2 (Wan Team, Alibaba 2025) (Alibaba, Feb 2025 / Jul 2025) — 14 B-param DiT with high-compression 3D VAE; Apache-2.0. Multilingual prompts (English + Chinese) work natively.
  • LTX-Video (HaCohen et al. 2024) (Lightricks, Nov 2024) — 2 B- param DiT optimised for speed: produces 5-second 768×512 clips in 4 seconds on an H100. Trades quality for inference latency in the same way SDXL Turbo did for images.
  • Open-Sora / Open-Sora-Plan (Zheng et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2024) (PKU + HuaWei + community, 2024–2025) — community reimplementations of the inferred Sora recipe. Useful for ablations and for understanding what a high-quality DiT pipeline looks like end-to-end; not yet competitive with the curated commercial models.
  • Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) / SVD-XT / Stable Video 4D (Blattmann, Dockhorn, et al. 2023) (Stability AI, 2023–2024) — the U-Net line. SVD-XT generates 25-frame clips; Stable Video 4D adds multi-view generation (4 views × 8 frames). Still widely used for image-to-video where the input is a still image.

The convergence in 2025–2026 is striking: every open generator uses a DiT with a 3D-VAE latent, T5-XXL or LLM text conditioning, and trains on a filtered re-captioned video corpus (closely mirroring the data evolution of Section 14.9 in Chapter 14). The differences sit in (i) parameter count, (ii) attention layout (joint vs dual-stream), (iii) precise VAE compression ratio, and (iv) inference-time tricks (Mochi’s asymmetric attention, LTX’s speed-optimised solver).

15.6 Evaluation

Video generation evaluation is in a much worse state than video understanding evaluation, because there is no single “correct” output. The 2024–2026 reporting suite is:

Distributional metrics

  • Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) (Unterthiner et al. 2018) (2018). The video analogue of FID: compute I3D features for real and generated clips, fit Gaussians, measure 2-Wasserstein distance between them. Reported on UCF-101 and MSR-VTT. Strong correlation with human preference for content quality, weak correlation with temporal consistency.
  • Inception Score (IS) / Kinetics-FID / CLIP-FID — variants on the FID theme with different feature extractors. Less common in 2024–2026.

FVD has known issues — sensitive to clip length, frame rate, and the choice of I3D checkpoint — but it is the only reported number for nearly every paper, so it persists.

Compositional / dimension-decomposed metrics

  • VBench / VBench++ (Huang et al. 2023) (2024). The dominant evaluation suite for 2024–2026 generation papers. Breaks down generation quality into 16 dimensions — subject consistency, background consistency, temporal flickering, motion smoothness, dynamic degree, aesthetic quality, imaging quality, object class, multiple objects, human action, colour, spatial relationship, scene, appearance style, temporal style, overall consistency — each scored by a small dedicated evaluator (CLIP, ViCLIP, MUSIQ, RAFT, ViT-based classifiers). The headline number is a weighted mean; the per- dimension breakdown lets papers argue about which aspect their model is good at.
  • EvalCrafter (Liu et al. 2023) (2024). A similar dimension-decomposed benchmark with ~700 prompts × 17 metrics. Often reported alongside VBench.
  • VideoScore (He et al. 2024) (2024). A learned LLM-based evaluator trained on human preference data; emerging as a single- number sanity check.

Human preference

Side-by-side preference tests against a strong baseline (typically Sora, Veo, or CogVideoX-5B). Almost every 2025–2026 paper reports this in addition to FVD + VBench, because the automatic metrics still fail to discriminate among the top open models. The standard protocol: 50–100 prompts × 3–5 raters, blinded A/B comparison, report win rate.

Prompt sets

The standard ones:

  • VBench prompts (~950 prompts across the 16 dimensions).
  • EvalCrafter prompts (~700 prompts).
  • T2V-CompBench (K. Sun et al. 2024) — adversarial compositional prompts (e.g. “a red cube on top of a blue cylinder, rotating while a yellow ball orbits”); designed to expose compositional failures that pretty pictures hide.

Recommendations

For a 2026 video-generation paper:

  1. FVD on UCF-101 + MSR-VTT — legacy but expected.
  2. VBench (full 16 dimensions + headline mean) — the de facto 2024–2026 default.
  3. A human-preference A/B against a flagship — Sora 2, Veo 3, or CogVideoX 1.5-5B depending on what is fair (closed vs open weights).
  4. Sanity check on a compositional prompt set (T2V-CompBench or one of the VBench compositional sub-axes) — to confirm the model has not collapsed to “pretty but wrong”.

15.7 Open Problems and Future Directions

Brief, because the chapter is intentionally short.

  • Long-form video. Most open models cap out at 5–10 seconds. Sora showed 1 min, Veo 3 nominally supports 8 s, Movie Gen ~16 s — but coherent narrative over a minute is still rare. The open frontier is autoregressive chunked generation (generate \(k\) seconds, condition the next chunk on the last) vs one-shot long-context DiTs.
  • Controllability. Text alone is a weak control signal for video. Pose / depth / camera-trajectory control (Video-ControlNet, CameraCtrl), reference-image identity preservation, and structured edits (Movie Gen’s text-guided editing) are active research lines.
  • Physics. Top-of-leaderboard models still produce subtle physics violations (objects passing through each other, gravity glitches, fluid dynamics implausibilities). The 2025–2026 wave of world model generators (Genie 3, Cosmos) explicitly trades generality for physical plausibility.
  • Audio. Veo 3 and Movie Gen generate synced audio; Sora 2 does not yet. Joint video+audio generation is the most likely next flagship axis — the model needs to know which sound goes with which visible event.
  • Evaluation honesty. VBench is increasingly gameable — papers optimise for high VBench scores rather than for human preference. The community is working on more adversarial prompt sets (T2V-CompBench, Movie Gen Bench) but no clean replacement exists yet.

15.8 Summary

Video generation in 2024–2026 has converged on a tight stack: a 3D-VAE that compresses pixels to a low-dimensional latent, a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) that denoises that latent, and a T5-XXL or LLM text encoder that injects prompts via cross-attention. Within that stack, three families differ in how much video training they do — train from scratch (Sora, CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo), fine-tune from an image model (Make-a-Video, Stable Video Diffusion), or adapt for free (Text2Video-Zero) — but the architecture is increasingly the same. The autoregressive line (VideoPoet, Janus-Pro, MAGI/Cosmos) is the credible orthogonal alternative, especially for unified understanding+generation models.

Compared with video understanding (Chapter 12), the generation field is roughly 18 months behind on data quality and benchmark consolidation. There is no MVBench-equivalent for generation in 2026 — VBench is closest, but every paper has its own headline number and human preference is still the ground truth. As the infrastructure and data converge, expect the next two years to look much like the understanding side did in 2024: rapid architectural convergence, a stable evaluation suite, and a sharper focus on the remaining hard axes — long form, controllability, physics, and honest evaluation.

15.9 Interview Questions

Q: What is the core architectural difference between Sora/CogVideoX and earlier video diffusion models like VDM or Imagen Video?

VDM and Imagen Video use a U-Net denoiser with 3D convolutions and factorized spatiotemporal attention — spatial and temporal processing are largely separate operations. Sora and CogVideoX replace the U-Net with a Diffusion Transformer (DiT), tokenizing the video into 3D spacetime patches and applying full self-attention jointly across all spatial and temporal positions. This lets the same architecture handle variable-length, variable-resolution video (and degenerate to image generation at \(T=1\)), and benefits from transformer scaling laws — at the cost of higher per-step compute than the factorized U-Net pattern.

Q: Describe how Make-a-Video generates video without training a video diffusion model from scratch.

Make-a-Video inserts (2D+1) convolutional and attention layers into a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model: each 2D conv is followed by a temporal 1D conv initialized to the identity, and each spatial attention layer is followed by a temporal attention layer. The image weights stay frozen (or lightly fine-tuned); only the new temporal layers are trained on video data. Because the temporal layers initialize as identity functions, the model initially behaves exactly like the image model on individual frames — a stable starting point that preserves image generation quality while learning motion cheaply.

Q: Why does almost every video generator use a 3D VAE rather than the 2D VAE from image diffusion?

A 2D VAE compresses each frame independently, so a video tensor is still \(T\) times larger than a single image latent — the dominant cost for video diffusion. A 3D VAE compresses space and time jointly (e.g. \(8{\times}8{\times}4\)), giving an additional ~4× reduction along the temporal axis on top of the spatial compression. This is what makes it feasible to run a transformer or U-Net over the latent at all; without it, even a modest 16-frame clip would have a sequence length 16× larger than the equivalent image.

Q: VBench reports 16 separate dimensions instead of one number. Why, and what’s the risk?

Video quality is multi-axis: a model can have excellent per-frame aesthetics but flicker badly (low temporal consistency), or move very little because static frames score well on most metrics (low “dynamic degree”). A single averaged score hides these trade-offs and can be gamed — e.g., a model that barely moves scores well on subject/background consistency. The 16-dimension breakdown lets a paper show which axis it improves, but the flip side is that papers increasingly optimize directly for VBench sub-scores rather than for human preference, which is why human A/B testing remains mandatory alongside it.

Q: Compare the autoregressive and diffusion approaches to video generation. Why might unification with LLMs matter beyond raw quality?

Diffusion generates a clip by iterative denoising of a continuous (or VAE) latent and currently leads on raw visual quality (Sora, Veo 3, CogVideoX 1.5). Autoregressive generation tokenizes video into discrete VQ codes and predicts them one at a time, like text — currently behind on quality but improving rapidly with better tokenizers (MAGVIT-v2). The unification argument is architectural: an AR video model uses the same token interface as an LLM, so a single transformer can both understand video (read tokens, write text — Chapter 11) and generate it (read text, write tokens), and inherits every LLM-serving optimization (speculative decoding, KV-cache reuse, prefix sharing) for free. This is why Janus-Pro and the broader “unified” model line bet on AR despite the current quality gap.

Q: How does classifier-free guidance behave differently for video versus image generation?

CFG blends conditional and unconditional denoiser outputs, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,\varnothing) + w[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{c}) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,\varnothing)]\), trading diversity for prompt fidelity. For video, the practical guidance scale is typically higher (6–9 vs. 4–7.5 for images) because the temporal-consistency requirement makes under-conditioned samples noticeably worse — a video that drifts off-prompt over time looks like a different video halfway through, which is far more jarring than a single off-prompt image. Over-guidance also has a video-specific failure mode: oversaturated, low-motion (“frozen”) clips, since high guidance pushes toward the conditional mode at the expense of the diversity that produces natural motion.
